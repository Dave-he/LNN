QUERY-CONDITIONED LIQUID TEMPORAL GROUNDING FOR LONG-FORM AUDIO
Yuelan Cheng1, Yonggang Zhu3, Yongqiang Chen1, Peiwei Chang1, Yingyi Chen2, Wenwu Wang1
1Centre for Vision, Speech and Signal Processing, University of Surrey, UK
2College of Information and Electrical Engineering, China Agricultural University, China
3School of Artificial Intelligence, Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications, China
ABSTRACT
Long-form temporal audio grounding (TAG) is challenging
because target events often occupy only a small portion of a
recording, while queries may impose multiple semantic con-
straints over a large temporal search space. We propose a
query-conditioned liquid temporal grounding framework that
injects query semantics through FiLM before a closed-form
continuous-time (CfC) network models long-range temporal
dependencies. Multiple query-relevant candidates are gen-
erated, from which a frozen large audio-language model se-
lects the most compatible one for lightweight local boundary
refinement. The pretrained backbone remains frozen, with
only lightweight grounding modules optimized. Experiments
on the English TAG subset of LAT-Bench show consistent
improvements across MOSS-Audio and Qwen3-Omni, while
ablations confirm the complementary roles of FiLM and CfC.
Index Terms—Temporal audio grounding, long-form au-
dio, query conditioning, liquid neural networks, large audio-
language models
1. INTRODUCTION
Temporal audio grounding (TAG) aims to localize the tem-
poral boundaries of audio content described by a natural-
language query [1, 2]. Early TAG studies focus on learning
fine-grained correspondences between textual descriptions
and localized acoustic events, while recent audio-language
pretraining methods further improve fine-grained audio-text
alignment [3, 4]. Long-form TAG extends temporal ground-
ing to substantially longer recordings, introducing greater
localization difficulty. The target segment may occupy only
a small fraction of the recording and can be surrounded by
acoustically or semantically similar content [5, 6]. Mean-
while, the grounding query may contain multiple semantic
constraints involving speakers, events, attributes, and tem-
poral relations [5]. As illustrated in Fig. 1, long-form TAG
requires localizing short target segments corresponding to po-
tentially complex queries over a substantially larger temporal
search space. Recent studies also report substantial perfor-
mance degradation as audio duration increases and show that
precise temporal localization remains challenging for current
audio-language models [7, 5, 6].

Existing approaches improve temporal audio understand-
ing through explicit temporal encoding, grounding supervi-
sion, or global-to-local reasoning [7, 8, 5]. However, long-
form TAG requires searching for query-relevant events over
a large temporal space, motivating temporal modeling that is
directly guided by the query. To this end, we propose a query-
conditioned liquid temporal grounding framework. FiLM [9]
first injects query semantics into temporal audio representa-
tions, which are then modeled by a closed-form continuous-
time (CfC) network [10]. Here, “liquid” refers to the input-
adaptive continuous-time dynamics of CfC, allowing tempo-
ral evolution to depend on the query-conditioned representa-
tions. The resulting temporal evidence is used to generate
multiple candidate intervals, from which a frozen LALM se-
lects the most semantically compatible one. A lightweight
local refiner further adjusts its boundaries to obtain the final
prediction. The pretrained audio-language backbone remains
frozen throughout training, and only the proposed temporal
grounding modules are optimized, enabling training on a sin-
gle NVIDIA A100 GPU while maintaining competitive local-
ization performance.
Experiments on the English TAG subset of LAT-Bench [5]
demonstrate consistent improvements with both MOSS-
Audio [11] and Qwen3-Omni [12] backbones. Ablation
studies demonstrate a strong interaction between FiLM-
based query conditioning and CfC-based temporal modeling.
Lightweight boundary refinement provides additional gains
in localization precision.

2. PROPOSED METHOD
2.1. Framework Overview
As illustrated in Fig. 2, our framework performs query-
conditioned temporal grounding over long-form audioA.
Instead of directly regressing a single temporal interval
from the full recording, the model first preserves mul-
tiple query-relevant temporal hypotheses through query-
conditioned temporal modeling. A frozen large audio-
language model (LALM) [12, 11] then selects the candidate
that is most semantically compatible with the query, followed
by lightweight local boundary refinement to obtain the final
predictionˆτ= (ˆs,ˆe).
2.2. Query-Conditioned Temporal Modeling
The audio encoder first produces high-resolution audio rep-
resentations, which are temporally projected and pooled into
H= [h 1, . . . ,hT ]. The query encoder produces token-level
representationsQ= [q 1, . . . ,qL]and a pooled query repre-
sentationq.
SinceHis initially query-independent, we first in-

The query-conditioned sequence ˜H= [ ˜h1, . . . ,˜hT ]is
then processed by a closed-form continuous-time (CfC) net-

directly influence long-range temporal information propaga-
tion rather than only downstream candidate scoring.

Training objective.The temporal predictor is trained to
estimate boundary locations, identify relevant regions, and
learn audio–query correspondence. The boundary lossL bnd

the matching signalp m, a hard-negative lossL match encour-

interval and an end-boundary hard-negative lossL ehn to

endpoint.

They do not directly supervise candidate-level ranking; se-

frozen LALM.
2.4. LALM-Based Candidate Selection
For each candidate intervalc i = (s i, ei), the frozen LALM
independently evaluates the corresponding audio crop to-
gether with the original queryQusing a Yes/No compatibil-
ity prompt. We apply a two-way softmax to the next-token
logits forYesandNo, and use the probability assigned toYes
as the candidate’s compatibility score. The highest-scoring
candidate is selected, yielding the interval˜τ= (˜s,˜e).
2.5. Local Boundary Refinement
For the Qwen3-Omni system, we apply a lightweight local
refiner to the LALM-selected candidate˜τ= (˜s,˜e). A local
window extending 5 seconds beyond both candidate bound-
aries is encoded through two complementary paths: a frozen
Qwen3-Omni audio encoder provides semantic representa-
tions, while a lightweight Conv1D encoder operating on the
mel spectrogram provides higher-resolution acoustic features.
Both representations are query-conditioned by FiLM, fused,
and pooled around the candidate start, center, and end to ob-
tain a local boundary representation.
The refiner predicts bounded residual corrections and
gates,∆b=δ max tanh(rb)andα b =σ(g b), forb∈ {s, e},
whereδ max = 5seconds. The refined boundaries are com-
puted as ˆb= clip( ˜b+α b∆b,0, D), yielding the final interval
ˆτ= (ˆs,ˆe).
The refiner is trained withL ref =λ regLreg +λ IoULIoU,
whereL reg is a SmoothL1 loss on the bounded pre-gate
boundary residuals andL IoU = 1−IoU([ˆs,ˆe],[s∗, e∗]). Can-

excluded during training, since such targets fall outside the
correction range of the local refiner.

3. EXPERIMENTS
Datasets.We conduct experiments on the English TAG data
fromListening with Time[5]. For training, we use the En-
glish TAG subset of LAT Chronicle, containing 3,737 query–
segment pairs, with a 9:1 train–validation split. Evaluation is
conducted on the English TAG subset of LAT-Bench, which
contains long-form recordings of up to 30 minutes with cor-
responding grounding queries.
Evaluation Metrics.Following the official LAT-Bench pro-
tocol, we report mean intersection over union (mIoU) and Re-
call@IoU at thresholds of 0.3, 0.5, and 0.7.
Implementation Details.We use frozen MOSS-Audio-8B-
Instruct [11] and Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B-Instruct [12] as
backbones. The temporal grounding modules are trained for
three epochs using AdamW with a learning rate of1×10 −4
over 1,024 temporal bins. The loss weights in Eq. (5) are
set to(0.3,1.0,0.5,0.5,0.3). For Qwen3-Omni, the LALM-
selected Top-1 candidate is further processed by a lightweight
boundary refiner trained on a±5s local window. All experi-
ments are conducted on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU.

4.3. Ablation Study
We ablate FiLM and CfC on both audio-language backbones
and further replace CfC with a standard GRU to examine
the role of liquid temporal modeling. To isolate the effects
of these components, all ablation experiments use the same
ρg=0.65candidate-generation configuration. The results are
reported in Table 3.
The full FiLM+CfC configuration achieves the highest
mIoU on both backbones. FiLM alone does not improve
mIoU over using neither component, whereas adding FiLM
to CfC increases mIoU from 37.21 to 38.52 on MOSS-Audio
and from 38.37 to 42.72 on Qwen3-Omni. This suggests that
query conditioning becomes more effective when coupled
with CfC-based liquid temporal modeling.
More importantly, replacing CfC with a standard GRU re-
duces mIoU to 32.00 on MOSS-Audio and 31.29 on Qwen3-
Omni. The gain therefore cannot be explained simply by
introducing recurrent temporal modeling. Instead, the results
are consistent with the benefit of the continuous-time, input-
adaptive dynamics provided by CfC for modeling query-
conditioned temporal evolution in long-form audio.
5. CONCLUSION
We presented a query-conditioned liquid temporal ground-
ing framework for long-form audio using FiLM, CfC, frozen
LALM candidate selection, and lightweight boundary re-
finement. Experiments on LAT-Bench show consistent im-
provements across MOSS-Audio and Qwen3-Omni, while
ablations confirm the complementary roles of FiLM and CfC.
The multi-candidate design further preserves diverse tem-
poral hypotheses, and local refinement improves boundary
precision after candidate selection. With frozen backbones
and lightweight trainable modules, the framework supports
single-GPU training. The Top-1–oracle gap motivates future
work on stronger candidate selection.
