Abstract
Synthesizing programs from execution traces is a fundamental challenge in al-
gorithmic interpretability and the reverse engineering of complex systems. We
bring a rough-path-theoretic perspective to program induction, treating execution
traces as high-dimensional rough paths and encoding them with the path signature
transform, a tool from stochastic analysis whose expectation provably characterizes
the distribution of traces a program induces. On three program-induction domains,
Karel, List, and RASP, our log-signature trace encoder outperforms LSTM, Trans-
former, and Fourier baselines in almost every setting, with its largest margins when
training data is scarce. We further observe that the trace-to-program pipeline is
naturally contractive: it often produces valid programs that are shorter than the
ground truth. We turn this into a bootstrapping procedure that exposes redundancy
in the ground-truth supervision without any external oracle, which we demonstrate
on redundancy-injected versions of our datasets. Finally, the log-signature encoder
induces a markedly more interpretable latent geometry than the baselines, in which
the nearest neighbors of every state lie on its own execution trace.

1 Introduction
Program synthesis, the task of automatically generating code that satisfies a given specification,
stands as one of the central challenges in modern artificial intelligence. While much of the recent
progress has focused on synthesizing programs from functional specifications, typically provided
as input-output (I/O) pairs [8, 1, 2, 4], this represents only one facet of the problem. A distinct but
equally important challenge is synthesizing programs from behavioral specifications in the form
of execution traces. This task is commonly referred to as program induction. Unlike I/O pairs,
which treat the program as a black box, execution traces reveal the how of the computation, offering
a granular view of the algorithmic decision-making process. Mastering this form of synthesis is
essential for real-world applications such as reverse-engineering legacy systems, malware analysis,
and algorithmic interpretability, where the goal is not just to replicate an output, but to recover the
underlying logic that produced a specific sequence of states.
This direction is essential for algorithmic interpretability, as it allows us to distill opaque agents
into human-readable code [30]. By synthesizing programs that faithfully reproduce the trace of a
policy rather than just its final output, we gain verifiable insights into agent behavior, revealing safety
constraints and logic gaps that are otherwise hidden in high-dimensional weight matrices. Towards
this end, it is compelling to find more optimal methodologies for encoding behavioral traces. Unlike
static I/O pairs, behavioral traces possess intrinsic temporal and geometric structure that characterizes
how a task is solved, not just what the solution is. Consequently, the success of the synthesis process
hinges on the representation’s ability to capture these dynamics robustly.

To tackle this task, the dominant paradigm has been to treat execution traces as discrete sequences,
processing them with standard sequence models like LSTMs [13] or Transformers [29]. In this work,
we propose a fundamental shift in perspective. Rather than viewing traces merely as sequences of
tokens, we conceptualize them as high-dimensional rough paths and encode them using the path
signature transform, a tool from stochastic analysis and rough path theory [3] that has been of growing
interest in machine learning for time-series forecasting and reinforcement learning [5, 15]. Under
this lens, a program is a generator of rough paths: each execution traces out a curve in the latent state
space whose geometric shape reflects the underlying control flow. The signature transform turns
that geometry into a fixed-size, length-invariant embedding with a principled algebraic structure,
providing a representation that is naturally aligned with how programs structure execution.
Several properties of the signature reinforce this perspective. First, the expected signature charac-
terizes the trace distribution a program induces [6], so aggregating signatures across executions
of the same program can, in principle, identify it. Second, signatures factor under any temporal
decomposition of the path: by Chen’s identity, S(X ∗ Y ) = S(X) ⊗ S(Y ), so the signature of a
trace is the product of the signatures of its parts (Section 4.2). Third, the signature captures the shape
of the path in a fixed-size representation regardless of path length. Fourth, its runtime is linear in the
path length, making it much more practical for processing long traces than Transformers with their
quadratic attention complexity (Appendix A.5). Beyond these four points, the signature is a universal
nonlinearity: any continuous functional of the path can be linearly approximated from its signature,
so no expressive power is sacrificed for these structural advantages. This last property notably does
not hold for classical operators like the Fourier transform.
Contributions. We make the following key contributions:
(i) We explore the use of path signature methods for learning programmatic behavior, bringing a
rough-path-theoretic perspective into program induction. On three program-induction domains,
our log-signature trace encoder outperforms LSTM, Transformer, and Fourier baselines in
almost every setting, with its largest margins when training data is scarce.
(ii) We show that our trace-to-program pipeline is naturally contractive: it produces shorter valid
programs than the ground-truth program in a sizable fraction of cases. We turn this property
into a bootstrapping procedure that progressively replaces ground-truth training programs
with model-generated ones whenever they are valid and shorter, and use it as a measurement
instrument that exposes redundancy in the supervision without any external oracle.
(iii) We show that the log-signature encoder induces a markedly more interpretable latent geometry
than the baselines: the states of each execution trace form a separate chain, and the nearest
neighbors of every state lie on its own trace.
2 Related work
Path signature in deep learning. Early applications utilized the truncated signature as a static
feature vector for standard supervised learning tasks, such as handwriting analysis and financial
time-series classification [17, 11]. It has also been used to summarize trajectories in non-Markovian
reinforcement learning as an agent memory [15]. More recently, Neural Rough Differential Equations
[21] have used the log-signature transform as the driving control of a continuous-time architecture for
long time series, embedding signature theory directly into the model’s dynamics rather than treating
it as a fixed feature map. A common thread between these methods is that they aim to extract some
information from the signature features. In contrast, we use the signature of a behavioral trace not
only to summarize it but also to characterize and reconstruct the generating process, i.e., the program.
Neural program induction. This paradigm leverages the insight that traces provide a denser
supervisory signal than I/O pairs, reducing the search space for complex algorithms. Many existing
works learn the programs in the latent weights of the model [22, 10, 32]. This has the downside of
losing interpretability over the learned programs. Reed and de Freitas [25] introduced the Neural
Programmer-Interpreter (NPI), which learned to execute programs by composing sub-programs,
trained to imitate full execution traces, though it required expensive step-by-step supervision. To
relax this requirement, Shin et al. [26] proposed a two-stage trace-inference framework, where a
model first predicts a latent execution trace from I/O pairs and subsequently decodes the program

Key properties and invariances. The path signature possesses several algebraic and geometric
properties [5] that make it a highly desirable feature map for machine learning tasks. Firstly, the
signature map is injective for paths up to tree-like equivalence [12]. Essentially, S(X) uniquely
determines the path X (up to “backtracking”), ensuring that no information regarding the shape of
the trajectory is lost during the transformation. Secondly, the signature is a universal nonlinearity:
linear functionals on the signature can approximate any continuous function of the path arbitrarily
well. This allows linear models (like linear regression) trained on signature features to learn complex,
nonlinear relationships in the original data space.
Truncation and log-signature. The full signature is an infinite-dimensional object. For compu-
tational feasibility, we compute the truncated signature up to level M , denoted as SM (X). The
dimension of the feature vector for a path in Rd truncated at level M is PM ℓ=1 dℓ, which grows geometrically with M.
To mitigate this dimensionality explosion, we utilize the log-signature log(S(X)). The logarithm
of the signature maps the elements from the tensor algebra into the underlying Lie algebra. The
log-signature is a more compact representation that retains the same information as the signature (up
to tree-like equivalence) but removes the redundant terms generated by the shuffle product.
The dimension of the log-signature at level ℓ is given by Witt’s formula, which is significantly smaller
than dℓ. For example, while the signature of a path in R2 truncated at M = 4 has 30 terms, the
log-signature has only 8.

4 Approach
We propose an encoder-decoder architecture for synthesizing programs from execution traces. Our
pipeline consists of three key networks: (1) a state encoder ϕ that embeds the semantic state of the
environment, where each state embedding is a d-dimensional point on the latent trace. Next, we use
(2) a trace encoder that embeds state sequences into a single representation that gets passed to (3) a
Transformer-based program decoder to synthesize the trace-generating program. Our trace encoder
is based on the path log-signature transform, which captures the geometric properties of the state
sequence. An overview of our architecture is shown in Figure 1. Note that Karel grids are just one
example of a domain to which our approach applies; the same architecture and training procedure are
used for the List and RASP domains.
4.1 Problem formulation
We consider the general task of trace-to-program synthesis: given a set of execution traces produced by
an unknown program, recover a program in a target domain-specific language (DSL) that reproduces
them. Formally, fix a DSL P and a runtime environment E that endows P with a state space S and an
execution semantics: running a program ρ ∈ P from an initial state s0 ∈ S produces a trace
τ = E(ρ, s0) = (s1, s2, . . . , sT ), st ∈ S, (4)
where T is the (program- and state-dependent) execution length. Given a set of traces T =
{τ1, . . . , τK } produced by a hidden program ρ∗ from initial states {sk,0}K k=1, the goal is to learn
a mapping T 7→ ˆρ such that E(ˆρ, sk,0) = τk for every k. Different choices of (P, E) instantiate
different program-induction domains. Throughout, we use τk = (sk,1, . . . , sk,Tk ) to denote the k-th
trace and Tk its length.
We illustrate the formulation with the Karel [24] and List DSLs (Figure 2), which share an iden-
tical control-flow skeleton (sequencing, conditionals, bounded repetition, unbounded loops over a
perception predicate) and differ in their state-dependent perceptions and primitive actions. RASP
programs (Figure 2c) have no intermediate states, so there s0 = (x, p) is an input x and a position p,
and st = (x(t), ρ(x(t))), with x(t) the input x with its p-th token set to t.
Each state sk,t is mapped to a dense embedding vk,t ∈ Rd by a domain-specific encoder ϕ. For
grid-based domains such as Karel, ϕ is a Convolutional Neural Network applied to the visual grid;
for symbolic domains such as list manipulation, ϕ is a small elementwise embedder; for RASP, it is a
linear map followed by a residual MLP. In all cases, each trace τk is transformed into a sequence of
latent vectors Vk = (vk,1, . . . , vk,Tk ).
4.2 Why signatures match programs
Encoding each trace by its (log-)signature, rather than by a learned recurrent or attention-based
summary, is the central design choice of our approach. Two foundational results from rough path
theory ground this choice.

First, a program ρ is determined, up to behavioral equivalence, by the distribution µρ it induces over
execution traces, with the randomness coming from the choice of initial state. Chevyrev and Lyons
[6] show that the expected signature EX∼µρ [S(X)] uniquely characterizes µρ whenever it has an
infinite radius of convergence, which holds automatically for bounded-length traces such as ours.
Aggregating per-trace signatures across multiple executions of the same program therefore estimates
an object that, in principle, suffices to identify the generating program.
Second, by Chen’s identity [3], the signature is multiplicative under any temporal split of a path:
S(X[0,T ]) = S(X[0,t]) ⊗ S(X[t,T ]) for any t ∈ [0, T ]. (5)
This is an algebraic property of paths, not of program syntax: traces whose embedded paths share
a contiguous sub-segment (e.g., the same loop body iterated, the same inner subroutine called)
share a corresponding signature factor, so structurally similar executions land in structurally similar
regions of the embedding space. As such, signatures respect the temporal structure of any single
execution, equipping the embedding manifold with closed-form algebraic structure under temporal
sub-decomposition.
By contrast, none of the baseline encoders has a closed-form law relating the embedding of a trace
to those of its parts: an LSTM’s embedding depends on state order through a fixed recurrence, a
Transformer encoder recovers order only because its positional encoding breaks the permutation
equivariance of self-attention, and the truncated Fourier spectrum of a concatenation cannot be
recovered from the spectra of its parts.
4.3 Trace encoding via signature-based transforms
Embedding a sequence given the embeddings of its elements (states in our case) typically involves
a choice of an aggregation scheme, such as recurrence in LSTMs and pooling as well as [CLS]
tokens for Transformers. In practice, this choice is nontrivial and depends on the task at hand. For
example, recurrent aggregation famously suffers from forgetting and vanishing gradients in long
sequences. The signature is a natural aggregator: it outputs a fixed-size embedding regardless of the
sequence length, and its runtime and memory complexities are linear in the sequence length rather
than quadratic as in Transformers.
A more fundamental difference that sets the signature apart from LSTM and Transformer encoders is
that it operates on geometry while the latter operate on content. The signature captures the geometric
shape of the path traced out by the sequence of state embeddings, while LSTMs and Transformers
learn a content-based summary. We argue that programmatic behavior is fundamentally geometric:
the control flow of a program structures the execution into subroutines, loops, and conditionals that

Positional augmentation. Standard path signatures satisfy tree-like equivalence, meaning they
are invariant to backtracking (e.g., a sequence of actions like turnRight, turnLeft that returns to
the original state might be nullified). To resolve this and preserve the distinct execution history, we
augment the state embeddings with positional encodings. We define a time-augmented path sequence
˜Vk where:
˜vk,t = vk,t + PE(t) (6)
Here, PE(t) ∈ Rd is a fixed sinusoidal positional encoding. Because PE(t) differs at every step, an
action sequence that returns to an earlier state reaches a different point of the augmented path, so
it no longer forms a backtracking segment that the signature ignores. In many standard signature
applications, time is instead added as an extra channel, which rules out tree-like equivalence altogether.
In our setting, we found that additive positional encodings work better on Karel and List domains.
On RASP, where step t of every trace sets the swept token to t, the log-signature encoder instead
appends a one-hot encoding of t.

Xk : [0, Tk] → Rd. To handle variable-length traces within the batch, we pad ˜vk by repeating the

introduces an artifact in the form of a false increment at the end of the path.

5 Experiments
5.1 Datasets
We evaluate trace-to-program synthesis on three complementary program-induction domains: Karel,
a 2D grid-world programming language with visual state, List, a symbolic list-manipulation language,
and RASP, a language for expressing Transformer computations as sequence operations. Together
they cover a broad slice of the program-induction problem space: Karel is representative of visual
and spatial-reasoning domains (robotics control, tile-based puzzles, agent navigation), List is repre-
sentative of symbolic array- and string-manipulation domains (sorting, searching, counting, sequence
transformations), and RASP is representative of functional sequence-to-sequence programs. For
Karel and List we adopt a similar data generation process to that of Trivedi et al. [28], with 100K
training and 2500 test samples. To mitigate overfitting during training, we randomly sample 5 out of
6 traces for each program, and use all 6 traces during testing. An output program is marked as correct
if it reproduces all input traces when executed on their respective initial states.
RASP. RASP [31] composes elementwise maps (MAP), positional shifts (SHIFT), thresholds (CMPC),
and selector-width counts (HIST, COUNT) over a token sequence. RASP programs can be compiled
into Transformer weights [18], and Transformers can be trained so that they convert into human-
readable programs [9]. We use programs of nesting depth at most two (Figure 2c) on length-16
sequences of tokens in {1, . . . , 18}, one per distinct non-constant behavior: 5,816 for training and
2,500 for testing, so every test program computes an unseen function. A trace is a sweep: from a
random input, the token at one position is set to 1, . . . , 18 in turn, and each step is the pair of input
and output sequences, a point in R32. Each program has K=8 sweeps at distinct positions, of which
we sample 4 during training and use all 8 for testing; redrawing the sweeps of the training programs
gives 32K training samples. A decoded program is correct if it reproduces every output of every
sweep.

Redundancy-injected datasets. Bootstrapping (Section 4.5) can only shorten a label if a shorter
program reproduces the same traces, and our datasets contain little such redundancy: on the Karel
and List test splits, a greedy search over trace-preserving local edits shortens about a quarter of the
programs but removes only 4.5% and 5.2% of their tokens. To highlight the contractive effect of
our pipeline, we therefore build redundancy-injected versions of the three datasets, denoted Karel+,
List+, and RASP+, by rewriting the ground-truth programs. In each split, half of the programs are
rewritten with extra structure that leaves their actions (Karel, List) or outputs (RASP) unchanged on
every input, so injected tokens make up about 20% of the total. For Karel and List, the rewrites invert
standard simplification rules, e.g. S → REPEAT 1 (S) or S → IFELSE c (S) ELSE (S). For RASP, we
rewrite one categorical operation into an equivalent COMBINE of two, e.g. CMPC EQ c into COMBINE
min (CMPC GEQ c) (CMPC LEQ c), allowing COMBINEs to nest. The traces are unchanged, and
we use these datasets only for the bootstrapping experiment (Finding #2).
5.2 Baselines
We compare our log-signature trace encoder against three classical baselines that differ from it only
in the trace encoder, up to one minior exception for Fourier (Appendix A.7).
LSTM. We pass the state embeddings as the input sequence to an LSTM [13], and the last hidden
state is taken as the trace embedding. Although the temporal order is implicit in LSTM recurrence, we
found that adding sinusoidal positional encoding to the input state sequence improved its performance.
Transformer. We use a 2-layer Transformer encoder [29] with 8 self-attention heads and sinusoidal
positional encoding added to the input sequence. We found that adding more layers did not signifi-
cantly improve performance, and resulted in a collapse in the case of 8 or more layers. We obtain the
trace embeddings by mean-pooling over the output state embeddings.
Fourier transform. We compute the truncated orthonormal real-to-complex DFT [7] of the (posi-
tionally augmented) state sequence, retaining the m=9 lowest-frequency modes and stacking their
real and imaginary parts into the trace embedding. Full equations are given in Appendix A.6.
5.3 Hyperparameters
All models are trained for 100 epochs with a batch size of 32 and an embedding size of 256 (d=32
for the RASP log-signature encoder). We use the Adam optimizer [16] with a learning rate of 10−4
(details in Appendix A.7). We clip the global gradient norm at 0.5 [23]. The training pipeline is
implemented using PYTORCH, including a PYTORCH-native reimplementation of SIGNATORY [14]
for differentiable log-signature computations, and runs on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU.
5.4 Results
We organize our findings around three claims, each stated up front and then supported by the
corresponding evidence. We report semantic accuracy: the fraction of test programs whose decoded
program is correct in the sense of Section 5.1, i.e., it reproduces every given trace (Karel, List) or
output (RASP), whether or not it matches the ground-truth program token for token.

Finding #3: The log-signature encoder induces a more interpretable latent geometry. We

6 Conclusion
Summary. In this paper, we brought a rough-path-theoretic perspective to program induction,
exploring the path log-signature transform as a trace encoder for trace-to-program synthesis. Our
results have shown an inherent advantage of the log-signature encoder over traditional sequence
models. However, this is not to say the signature transform should necessarily be treated as a
complete replacement for other sequence encoders, but rather to establish grounds for future research
in employing geometric methods for program learning. The essence of this work is demonstrating
that geometry potentially reveals more about programmatic structure than content. Furthermore,
we have empirically shown that the trace-to-program pipeline is naturally contractive, which, as
our experiments on redundancy-injected datasets show, serves as a convenient tool for exposing
redundancy without any external oracle. Finally, we have shown that the log-signature encoder
induces a markedly more interpretable latent geometry than classical sequence models.
Limitations. The truncated log-signature provides a compact representation for moderately complex
traces, but the dimensionality of the feature space Dlogsig still grows exponentially with the truncation
level M . We keep M small in practice (Appendix A.1), but very-long-horizon programs that require
fine-grained higher-order interactions across the full trace remain a challenging case for the approach.

A.1 Signature truncation depth ablation
The truncation level M controls the maximum interaction order captured by the log-signature: M =1
retains only the displacement of the path, M =2 adds pairwise interactions (Lévy areas), and higher
M captures progressively higher-order shape statistics at exponential dimensional cost. We jointly
sweep M ∈ {1, 2, 3} and the underlying state embedding dimension d ∈ {16, 32, 64} at half of each
domain’s training data volume, and report semantic accuracy on the standard 2500-sample test set.
Figure 5 shows that M =1 falls far short of M =2 on every domain and dimension, and stays near
zero on Karel and RASP, confirming that pairwise interactions are the load-bearing component of the
representation. Going further to M =3 does not help on List and RASP, where it matches M =2 at
d=16 and is at or below it at larger d. On Karel, M =3 improves on M =2 at d=16 and d=64, but
not at d=32. Since M =3 multiplies the number of log-signature features by more than 40 at d=64
for a gain on one domain only, we use M =2 in the main experiments.

A.2 Signature versus log-signature
The log-signature is the compact alternative to the full signature: it removes the redundant terms
generated by the shuffle product. At M =2 these redundant terms make up half of the signature.
For a path with D channels, the signature has D + D2 = D(D+1) non-constant coordinates and
the log-signature D(D+1)/2. The extra coordinates are the symmetric part of the second level,
which by the shuffle identity equals 1 2 X(1) 0,T ⊗ X(1) 0,T , i.e. products of first-level coordinates, while its
antisymmetric part is the Lévy area that the log-signature keeps. To test whether these redundant
coordinates help the learned projection, we replace the log-signature with the signature at M =2,
keep every other setting of the main experiments, and train on each domain’s full training set (100K
samples for Karel and List, 32K for RASP). Figure 6 shows that doubling the number of features
leaves semantic accuracy essentially unchanged: the two encoders differ by at most 0.011 on any
domain, in either direction. Since the input of the projection doubles, the signature model is also
larger, with about 50% more parameters on Karel and List. The log-signature thus matches the
signature at half the representation size, which is why we use it throughout.

A.5 Encoder forward-pass wall-clock scaling
To check the linear-time claim empirically, we time the forward pass of each trace encoder on a single
trace of length T , up to T =105, on one NVIDIA A100 GPU. Each encoder has its configuration
from the Karel and List experiments, with state embeddings of width 256, and only the trace encoder
itself is timed, not the state encoder, the set encoder (Appendix A.7) or the program decoder around
it. Figure 9 shows that the log-signature and LSTM encoders grow linearly with T , while the
Transformer grows quadratically, as its attention compares every pair of steps. The Transformer
overtakes the LSTM at around 45,000 steps, and at T =105 it is about 18 times slower than the
log-signature encoder, which is in turn about 8 times faster than the LSTM. The Fourier encoder,
whose FFT is cheap at these lengths, is the fastest throughout.

A.7 Further optimization details
Set encoder. Before decoding, the K trace embeddings h1, . . . , hK of a program pass through a
set encoder, a 2-layer Transformer encoder with 8 heads and no positional encoding, which lets each
trace embedding attend to the others. We treat it as part of the program decoder, whose cross-attention
reads the K outputs of the set encoder rather than H itself. The Fourier encoder is the one exception
and is trained without the set encoder on all three domains, since with it the Fourier model did not
learn on Karel at any training data volume.
Weight decay. All models are trained with Adam and without weight decay, except the LSTM on
RASP, which we train with AdamW [19] and a weight decay of 1.0 on its weight matrices. Without
weight decay, the RASP LSTM does not learn: its semantic accuracy stays at about 0.03 or below at
every state embedding width and training data volume we tried, and weight decay rescues it partly.
A.8 Compute resources
Every run uses a single NVIDIA A100 GPU. At half the training data volume, a training run takes
about 4 h on Karel, 1–2 h on List, and 0.5 h on RASP, and the time grows roughly in proportion to the
volume. A bootstrapping run takes about 10 h on Karel+, 5 h on List+ (with two runs sharing one
GPU), and 1–2 h on RASP+. The experiments reported in this paper total roughly 500 GPU-hours;
the full project, including preliminary and discarded configurations, used more.
