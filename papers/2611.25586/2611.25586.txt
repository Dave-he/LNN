ABSTRACT
Inferring the latent interaction graph from multivariate time series is fundamen-
tal to understanding complex dynamical systems in neuroscience, biology, and
physics. Despite recent progress in neural relational inference, most methods rely
on discrete-time modeling, which degrades under sparse and irregular observations
and tends to entangle multi-hop dependencies into spurious direct edges. We
show that a continuous-time formulation naturally decouples inference from any
fixed observation schedule, allowing gradient signals to be integrated continuously
between observations for both dynamics and structure learning. We propose NOGI
(Neural ODE-based Graph Inference), a framework that couples a deterministic
graph learner with an MPNN-based Neural ODE. The two modules are trained
end-to-end via the adjoint method. Across four simulated dynamical systems at
two scales, NOGI achieves the best structure recovery in 30 out of 32 evaluated
cases with highly competitive forecasting performance, remaining robust under
observation degradation. On the realistic Netsim fMRI benchmark, it attains an
average AUPRC of 0.90 (AUROC 0.96). These results suggest that explicitly
decoupling relational interactions from self-dynamics within a continuous-time
vector field provides a powerful mechanism for graph structure learning under
realistic observation conditions.
1 INTRODUCTION
Inferring the latent interaction graph from multivariate time series is a fundamental problem in network
science, with direct applications in neuroscience (Bassett & Sporns, 2017), systems biology (Marbach
et al., 2012), and physics (Timme & Casadiego, 2014). Relying solely on observed trajectories of
coupled objects, the goal is to recover the underlying network topology that governs their interactions,
without prior knowledge of the governing equations or the dynamical rules. While discrete-time
models dominate the literature on neural relational inference, the dynamical systems they aim to
model are almost always continuous.
In practice, observations are far from ideal. In fMRI-based brain network discovery (Tagliazucchi
et al., 2012), recordings can be sparse and irregularly sampled due to sensor delays; in gene regulatory
network reconstruction (Greenfield et al., 2010), expression profiles are often contaminated by
measurement noise and available at only a handful of time points. Recovering the interaction graph
under such conditions is critical for downstream scientific discovery, yet remains largely unsolved.
Existing approaches fall into three categories. Classical statistical methods such as Granger causal-
ity (Granger, 1969) and its nonlinear extensions (Tank et al., 2021) test whether one variable’s
history improves prediction of another, but multivariate conditioning is expensive and scales poorly.
Discrete-time neural methods, including NRI (Kipf et al., 2018) and AIDD (Zhang et al., 2022),
jointly learn graph structure and dynamics via differentiable graph sampling, but degrade under
sparse and irregular sampling; moreover, their step-wise modeling often introduces spurious edges by
mistakenly capturing multi-hop dependencies as direct connections. Continuous-time neural methods
such as NGM (Bellot et al., 2022) and SCOTCH (Wang et al., 2024) leverage Neural ODEs or SDEs

topology with dynamics representation, which limits interpretability, or rely on computationally

Our key insight stems from a general form of coupled dynamical system (Eq. 1), which naturally
isolates self-dynamics from topology-driven interactions, making the graph an explicit, learnable
component of the continuous-time vector field. An ODE formulation further decouples the model
from any fixed observation schedule, and the adjoint method accumulates gradient signal for each
candidate edge continuously between consecutive observations. The adjoint method uses less memory
than direct backpropagation through the solver, with memory requirements that remain constant as
the number of solver steps grows. Building on this, we propose Neural ODE-based Graph Inference
(NOGI), which couples two jointly trained modules: (i) a deterministic graph learner that directly
optimizes a continuous edge-weight matrix, and (ii) a graph-conditioned Neural ODE whose vector
field is parameterized by a message passing neural network, explicitly encoding the learned topology
into the dynamics. (Fig. 1).
Our contributions are as follows:
• We propose NOGI, a framework that jointly infers latent interaction graphs and continuous-time
dynamics by coupling a deterministic graph learner with a graph-conditioned Neural ODE, trained
end-to-end via the adjoint method.
• Across four simulated dynamical systems at two scales (N =10 and N =100), NOGI achieves
superior structure recovery in 30 out of 32 comparisons. Furthermore, on the Netsim fMRI
benchmark, it attains an average AUROC of 0.96 and AUPRC of 0.90 across 27 simulations.
• Evaluated under extreme observational constraints (including observation noise, sparse sampling,
and severe data missingness), NOGI outperforms both discrete-time and continuous-time baselines
across most tested conditions.
• The learned dynamics module successfully transfers across different network topologies and scales,
demonstrating that NOGI effectively decouples intrinsic dynamical rules from structural inference.

2.1 PROBLEM STATEMENT
Consider a coupled dynamic system with N objects, where the underlying connections can be
represented by a directed graph G = (V, E). V denotes the set of nodes, and E denotes the set of
edges. The adjacency matrix A ∈ {0, 1}N ×N encodes the interaction between nodes, where Aji = 1
indicates a directed edge from vj to vi. The time-varying feature of node vi at time t is defined as
xt i ∈ Rd where d is the feature dimension.
System dynamics govern the temporal evolution of node features and can often be formally
expressed as a system of Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs). For instance, the classic
Kuramoto system (Kuramoto, 1984) describes the synchronization of coupled oscillators via
dθi/dt = ωi + K PN j=1 Aji sin(θj − θi), i = 1, . . . , N , where θi and ωi are the phase and natural
frequency of node vi, respectively, and K denotes the constant coupling strength. The Kuramoto
model indicates that the evolution of phase θi is not only driven by the node’s own natural frequency
ωi, but also by accumulated phase differences from its neighbouring nodes. Following the same logic,
a generalized formulation of complex network dynamics (Gao & Yan, 2022) can be derived as:

2.2 MODEL
Our model consists of two components that are jointly trained end-to-end (Fig. 1): a Graph Learner
that infers the weighted adjacency matrix, and a Dynamic Learner that models the temporal evolution
using Neural ODEs. NOGI accepts the snapshot of the dynamic system Xt as input. The key design

principle is that the learned graph structure explicitly participates in the dynamics modeling through
message passing, creating a direct gradient path from prediction error to graph structure.
2.2.1 GRAPH LEARNER
Theoretically, for a complex system with N nodes, the search space of discrete directed graph
structures is 2N ×(N −1), making heuristic optimization practically infeasible for large-scale networks.
A common differentiable learning approach is to relax the binary entries in the adjacency matrix via
the Straight-Through Estimator (Franceschi et al., 2019) or Gumbel-Softmax sampling (Kipf et al.,
2018; Zhang et al., 2022), allowing for gradient-based optimization. While effective, they inject
stochasticity into the forward pass or require a temperature schedule, which we find to interact poorly
with the iterative ODE solver downstream.
We adopt a deterministic continuous relaxation, parameterizing the graph with a learnable matrix
W ∈ RN ×N ≥0 initialized from U (0, 1). Entries Wji denote relative continuous edge strengths rather
than probabilities. Non-negativity is enforced via projected gradient descent (Wji ← max(Wji, 0)).
We impose no upper bound on W to help the optimizer escape poor local optima in the non-convex
objective, relying instead on a sparsity regularizer to bound the magnitudes.

3 EXPERIMENTS
Our experiments are designed to answer the following questions:
• Q1: Can NOGI recover ground-truth topology from diverse dynamical systems? (§3.2)
• Q2: Does NOGI learn the true underlying dynamics of the system? (§3.3, §3.5)
• Q3: Is NOGI robust to observation degradation? (§3.4)
• Q4: Are the learned dynamics transferable across different network topologies? (§3.5)
• Q5: Can NOGI generalize to realistic benchmarks beyond synthetic systems? (§3.6)
3.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Dynamical systems. We evaluate on four synthetic coupled network systems that span a range of
complexity: Spring (coupled oscillators, d=4), Kuramoto (phase oscillators, d=1), FHN (FitzHugh–
Nagumo neurons, d=2), and MM (Michaelis–Menten gene regulation, d=1), where d denotes the
per-node state dimension. For each system we generate trajectories on two scales (N =10 and
N =100) over Erd˝os–Rényi (ER) and Barabási–Albert (BA) random graphs. Details of simulation
settings are provided in Appendix A.2.
Baselines. NOGI is compared against baselines from three categories: (i) Model-free Causal
Measures: Mutual Information (MI) and Partial Correlation (PC), non-parametric, learning-free
metrics that infer causality based on statistical dependencies; (ii) Universal Time Series Models:
MTGNN (Wu et al., 2020) and iTransformer (Liu et al., 2024), both general-purpose forecasting
models; only MTGNN learns an adaptive graph and is thus also evaluated on structure inference;
and (iii) Neural Relation Inference Methods, which jointly learn graph structure and dynamics:
the discrete-time NRI (Kipf et al., 2018) and AIDD (Zhang et al., 2022), and the continuous-
time NGM (Bellot et al., 2022) and SCOTCH (Wang et al., 2024), built on Neural ODEs/SDEs.
Among these, SCOTCH is additionally evaluated as a state-of-the-art baseline on the realistic Netsim
benchmark (§3.6).
Metrics. To bridge the gap between the continuous dataset-level learned weights W and binary
ground-truth A, we prioritize relative ranking and evaluate structure inference as a binary classifica-
tion task using Area Under the ROC Curve (AUROC) and Area Under the Precision–Recall Curve
(AUPRC). For forecasting dynamics, we report RMSE and MAE on the held-out test split.

systems across two scales (10/100) under both ER and BA network configurations. NOGI achieves
the highest or jointly highest scores in 30 out of 32 comparisons; at the more challenging N =100, it
ranks first in all eight settings. The only two exceptions arise on FHN–BA at N =10, where NGM

Turning to the baselines, MI and PC often attain high AUROC but low AUPRC, especially at N =100:
they separate true edges from non-edges on average but include many false positives among the
top-ranked edges. MI is the exception on MM, where pairwise dependence closely reflects the
topology. MTGNN learns an adaptive graph, but only as a latent dependency structure serving
prediction, with no mechanism tying it to the underlying interactions; its recovery remains near-
random. Among the discrete-time methods, AIDD is the strongest: it is nearly perfect on Spring
and MM, yet underperforms on Kuramoto at both scales and collapses on FHN at N =100, where its
AUPRC falls to 0.18 and 0.01. The continuous-time baselines are competitive on some systems at
N =10, but degrade markedly at N =100: NGM’s AUPRC exceeds 0.50 only on MM, and SCOTCH’s
falls to 0.02–0.37, while NOGI still holds up at this scale. The difference lies in how structure is
represented: NGM encodes the graph implicitly in its network weights and SCOTCH infers it
variationally, whereas NOGI learns an explicit graph decoupled from the dynamics. We examine the
contributions of individual design choices through the component ablations in Section 3.7.
Finally, NRI’s low scores reflect a task mismatch. As a variational autoencoder, it infers a separate
latent graph for each input trajectory. Our benchmark instead follows the protocol of NGM and
SCOTCH, where all trajectories share a single sparse graph. Under this setting, NRI’s VAE architec-
ture offers no advantage, and NOGI outperforms it in all 16 comparisons at N =10; NRI does not
scale to N =100 owing to its O(N 2) encoder cost.

ratios (SNR ∈ {30, 20, 15, 10, 5} dB). On Spring and MM, NOGI degrades most gracefully and
consistently outperforms both baselines, maintaining 0.84 AUPRC on Spring even at SNR=5 dB,

4 CONCLUSION
We presented NOGI, a framework for joint graph inference and continuous-time dynamics learning
that couples a deterministic graph learner with a graph-conditioned Neural ODE. Across synthetic and
realistic benchmarks, NOGI improves structure recovery and remains robust under sparse, irregular,
and noisy observations. These results suggest that separating self-dynamics from topology-driven
interactions is an effective way to make the graph an explicit, learnable component of the vector
field. Formulating this vector field in continuous time further decouples inference from any fixed
observation schedule and accumulates gradient signal for each candidate edge continuously between
observations.
Four structural limitations point to future work. First, NOGI assumes a static graph, which does
not hold in evolving systems. Second, its deterministic ODE formulation cannot capture intrinsic
stochasticity; incorporating latent SDEs (Wang et al., 2024) could model process noise and improve
structural identifiability. Third, the dense N × N edge representation incurs O(N 2) cost, which
limits scalability to very large networks; restricting the graph learner to a sparse set of candidate
edges could lift this bottleneck. Finally, because all edges share a single message function Ψm and
W is non-negative, NOGI assumes that every edge follows the same interaction mechanism, and
thus cannot distinguish an edge that promotes the activity of its target from one that suppresses it.
Learning a sign or a type label for each edge would relax this assumption.

A.1 EXTENDED RELATED WORK
Graph Structure Learning from Time Series. Inferring the underlying network topology from
observed time series of coupled dynamical systems is a core problem in network science. Classical
approaches based on Granger causality (Granger, 1969) and their nonlinear extensions (Tank et al.,
2021) examine whether the predictability of one variable can be enhanced by other variables. Applying
these methods to identify true direct causalities requires multivariate conditioning, which incurs a
prohibitive computational complexity growing up to the fourth power of the network size and triggers
a severe curse of dimensionality.
Some approaches incorporate stronger dynamical priors but impose restrictive assumptions: BP-
PGM (Li et al., 2022) is limited to belief propagation dynamics on probabilistic graphical models,
and Gaskin et al. (2024) requires the governing ODE to be known.
Other works aim to learn the underlying dynamics and network structure jointly. Neural Rela-
tional Inference (NRI) (Kipf et al., 2018) introduced a variational autoencoder (VAE) framework,
demonstrating strong performance on simulated physical systems. Subsequent work extends this
paradigm along several axes: DNRI (Graber & Schwing, 2020) allows the inferred graph to vary
over time, ACD (Löwe et al., 2022) amortizes the inference to generalize across unseen systems, and
AIDD (Zhang et al., 2022) proposes a more general framework with differentiable Gumbel-softmax
sampling. However, these methods invariably rely on discrete-time dynamics models, introducing a
fundamental mismatch with the continuous-time nature of the underlying interactions, which can
bias the learned network structure.
NGM (Bellot et al., 2022) addresses this mismatch by leveraging Neural ODEs to model continuous
system dynamics, naturally supporting irregular time series observations. It discovers sparse struc-
tures by applying adaptive group Lasso to per-node MLPs, thereby producing a representation in
which dynamics and graph topology are tightly entangled. Leveraging latent Stochastic Differential
Equations (SDEs), Wang et al. (2024) proposed SCOTCH, a Bayesian structure learning method
that offers robust theoretical guarantees for structural identifiability under intrinsic stochasticity.
Nevertheless, SCOTCH relies on variational inference over both graph structures and latent SDE
trajectories; each training step solves an augmented SDE to estimate the path-wise KL term, making
the training cost grow with the number of observed time points and solver steps. This limits its
scalability to long sequential data in practice.
In contrast, our proposed NOGI takes a much simpler and more direct approach: it optimizes the
adjacency matrix and models the system dynamics using a Neural ODE powered by a Message
Passing Neural Network. Furthermore, the adjoint method reduces memory use compared with direct
backpropagation through the ODE solver, as demonstrated in Appendix A.5.
Related efforts in spatio-temporal forecasting (Wu et al., 2019; 2020; Bai et al., 2020; Shang &
Chen, 2021) learn soft adjacency matrices for dynamic prediction rather than recovering discrete
13

===== PAGE 14 =====

Preprint
ground-truth topology. Causal discovery from time series (Lagemann et al., 2023; Runge et al., 2023;
Brown & Carlson, 2025) also infers inter-variable relationships from observations, but focuses on
causal relations rather than coupling topologies of dynamical systems. Graph structure learning for
node classification (Franceschi et al., 2019; Jin et al., 2020; Li et al., 2023) operates on static features
without dynamics modeling.
Neural Ordinary Differential Equations with Graph Structure. Neural ODEs (Chen et al.,
2018) parameterize continuous-time hidden state dynamics with a neural network, with the latent
ODE formulation naturally suited for time series modeling. Several works have combined Neural
ODEs with graph neural networks, broadly falling into two categories.
The first treats graph structure as given and focuses on improving GNN expressiveness through
continuous-depth propagation: GODE (Poli et al., 2019) generalizes discrete GNN layers to con-
tinuous dynamics; CGNN (Xhonneux et al., 2020) designs continuous message passing from the
perspective of graph diffusion equations; and GRAND (Chamberlain et al., 2021) further extends
this to graph diffusion PDEs with learnable, time-varying edge weights via attention. The second
category applies Graph Neural ODEs to spatio-temporal systems, e.g., traffic forecasting (Fang et al.,
2021) and physical simulation (Zeng et al., 2025). CF-GODE (Jiang et al., 2023) extends GraphODE
to continuous-time counterfactual inference in multi-agent systems via adversarial balancing, but
assumes the interaction graph is known a priori.
Across both categories, the graph structure is assumed to be known. Our method removes this
assumption by integrating differentiable graph structure learning into a Graph Neural ODE, jointly
recovering both the interaction topology and the system dynamics.

A.4 IMPLEMENTATION AND TRAINING DETAILS
NOGI uses adjoint sensitivity with the adaptive dopri5 solver on both the synthetic datasets and
the Netsim benchmark. For Table 1, the sparsity weight λ was set separately for each system
from the grid {10−9, 10−8, 10−7, 10−6, 10−5}. For Table 4, a single λ = 10−5 was used for all 27
evaluated simulations. The reported graph was taken from the final training epoch. We use two Adam
optimizers: one for the graph learner parameters (W) with learning rate αg = 10−2, and one for the
dynamic learner parameters (θ) with learning rate αd set per system. All neural network parameters
use Kaiming uniform initialization, and W is initialized from U (0, 1). After each gradient step, we
clamp Wji ≥ 0 to maintain the non-negativity constraint. The exact settings for each system are
provided in our released code.
Trajectories are segmented into single-step prediction samples, which are pooled and randomly split
into training (60%), validation (20%), and testing (20%) subsets. Forecasting metrics are computed
on the held-out test subset. Structure metrics instead compare the learned W against the ground-
truth adjacency; since W is a single dataset-level parameter shared by all trajectories rather than a
per-sample prediction, these scores are not tied to any split. All reported results are averaged over 3
independent runs with different random seeds. Models are trained on NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs with
24GB memory.
Sensitivity and ground-truth-free selection of λ. In practice, the underlying graph of a new
dataset is unknown, so λ cannot be selected using structure-recovery metrics. We therefore evaluate a
stability-based criterion that selects λ from cross-seed graph stability alone. For each seed, we retain
the top 4% of directed off-diagonal entries of |W| as that seed’s edge set; the stability score of a
candidate λ is the mean pairwise Jaccard similarity between these edge sets across seeds, and the
λ with the highest stability is selected. To validate this method, we apply it as a single fixed rule
over the same grid λ ∈ {10−9, 10−8, 10−7, 10−6, 10−5} to the four synthetic systems on ER graphs
with N =100 and to Sim3 of the Netsim benchmark, with three seeds per configuration. We load
the ground-truth adjacency only after all stability scores have been computed and the selection has
been frozen, solely to report the AUPRC of the selected λ. As shown in Table 7, sensitivity to λ is
system-dependent; nevertheless, the blindly selected λ attains an AUPRC comparable to the best
value in the sweep in all five settings. These results provide empirical support for the criterion, but

A.6 PARTIALLY OBSERVED STATES AND HIDDEN NODES
We evaluate structure recovery when state variables or entire nodes are unobserved (Table 9). For

For hidden nodes, we randomly remove 10% or 30% of the nodes from the observations and evaluate
only the edges among the remaining observed nodes, repeating each setting with three random node
subsets. Spring achieves perfect recovery in all three draws at both fractions. FHN is more variable,
with one of the three draws at 10% hidden nodes failing (AUPRCs of 0.95, 0.97, and 0.05).
