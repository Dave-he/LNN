Abstract
Time-series data are often sampled irregularly at high frequencies and exhibit long-
range dependencies, which makes long-horizon modelling difficult. Continuous-
time models such as neural controlled differential equations (NCDEs) and neural
rough differential equations (NRDEs) can handle irregular sampling, but they
scale poorly to long sequences. Selective state-space models (SSMs) such as
Mamba scale linearly with sequence length, but they provide limited recurrent
mixing across hidden dimensions within a single block. We propose LogSig-SSM
(Log-Signature Compression for State-Space Models), which first compresses
long multivariate time series into a shorter sequence of tokens using multi-scale
windowed log-signatures, and then processes these tokens with a selective SSM
backbone. LogSig-SSM is scalable and robust to irregular sampling, combining
log-signature tokens that capture higher-order cross-channel interactions with a
selective SSM that models long-range dependencies. The model also admits a
continuous-time interpretation as an NCDE/NRDE-style system driven by a log-
signature-based input, in which selectivity induces an input-dependent rescaling of
the latent dynamics. Across four benchmarks, namely long-sequence classification
on UEA, high-frequency physiological regression on PPG-DaLiA, multivariate
weather forecasting, and irregularly sampled clinical prediction on PhysioNet
Sepsis, LogSig-SSM outperforms or matches strong SSM and continuous-time
baselines while training up to 30× faster and using up to 37× less GPU memory
than Mamba on the longest sequences.1
1 Introduction
Time-series modelling has numerous real-world applications in domains such as healthcare [9] and
finance [16]. These applications typically involve signals, such as ECG or transaction prices, that are
irregularly sampled at high frequencies and contain information spanning long time scales. Processing
these long sequences creates memory and computational bottlenecks which motivate the development
of models that scale to long sequences while remaining reliable under irregular sampling rates. Two
widely-used approaches are continuous-time models and State-Space-Models (SSMs).
Continuous-time models such as Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (NODEs) [5] and Neural
Controlled Differential Equations (NCDEs) [19] treat observed time-series data as discrete-time

samples from an underlying continuous-time process. NCDEs operate on continuous interpolations
of the data and naturally accommodate irregularly-sampled observations. However, training and
inference can become costly on longer sequences because numerical solvers often require more
integration steps. To address this computational bottleneck, Neural Rough Differential Equations
(NRDEs) [25] and Log-NCDEs [38] employ the log-ODE method [6, 11] which summarises infor-
mation between time intervals with truncated log-signatures, enabling the solver to take fewer, larger
steps. Signature-based summarisation has also been adapted to improve computational efficiency and
handle irregular sampling in discrete models such as RNNs [20] and transformers [24].
In parallel, structured SSMs [4, 8, 14, 15, 31, 34] have emerged as an efficient alternative to attention-
based models. Unlike transformers, whose computational cost scales quadratically [36], modern
SSMs achieve linear-time processing, constant-memory inference, and efficient GPU-parallel training.
SSMs have also shown competitive performance across various sequence modelling tasks [21, 32, 33,
43]. Recent selective SSMs, such as Mamba [14], introduced input-dependent gating enabling the
model to filter context content rather than uniformly compressing it. Additionally, novel theoretical
work [7] has established that SSMs admit a continuous-time interpretation as linear controlled
differential equations (CDEs), connecting them to the continuous-time modelling framework.
However, recent empirical studies highlight two key limitations of Mamba. First, performance can
degrade on very long sequences, where Mamba-based models may underperform attention-based
baselines [37]. Existing approaches mitigate this limitation through token pruning or decimation
[40, 3]. Second, Mamba’s diagonal state transition admits no within-block mixing across hidden
dimensions. As Cirone et al. [7, Cor 4.4] show, the closure of a single Mamba block decomposes
as a sum of per-channel functionals. This means genuinely multivariate path functionals, such as
Lévy areas, which depend on the joint behaviour of two channels, lie outside this closure. Two
mitigations exist for this limitation, but come with costs. Stacking blocks with linear inter-block
mixing eventually recovers full expressivity (Prop. 4.5 in [7]), but only as both depth and hidden-
state size grow without bound, i.e. cross-channel structure is paid for in model size. Replacing
the diagonal state transition with richer structure [39, 35] avoids the depth cost, but at non-trivial
efficiency overhead (PD-SSM reports approximately 6× slower runtime than Mamba [35]). These
limitations motivate a complementary strategy: compressing the effective sequence length while
injecting explicit higher-order cross-channel information at the input, leaving the efficient diagonal
scan intact.
Log-signature features [23] provide exactly such input-level functionals. They encode iterated
integrals of the path, including Lévy areas, while inheriting invariance to monotone time reparame-
terisations and thus offering robustness to irregular sampling. Building on this idea, we introduce
LogSig-SSM, a two-stage architecture that achieves both accuracy and efficiency on long sequences.
First, a long multivariate sequence is mapped to a shorter token sequence by computing truncated
log-signatures over strided windows at multiple scales, yielding compact descriptors that explicitly
capture cross-channel dependencies. Second, these tokens are processed by a diagonal selective SSM,
which efficiently models long-range dependencies over the reduced sequence length.
Our contributions are:
• We introduce LogSig-SSM, a model that integrates log-signature tokenisation with selective
SSMs. The tokeniser compresses long, irregularly-sampled multivariate sequences into
compact summaries by computing truncated log-signatures over multi-scale windows.
• We provide a continuous-time interpretation of LogSig-SSM by extending the linear-CDE
view of SSMs [7], interpreting the model as an NCDE/NRDE-style system driven by
log-signature-derived controls with input-dependent time rescaling of the latent dynamics.
• We establish a depth–truncation tradeoff. A single LogSig-SSM block followed by an MLP
can approximate any continuous functional of the windowed log-signature up to depth m,
while the same class of functionals lies outside the closure of a single diagonal Mamba
block (even with a pointwise MLP, which cannot recover features absent from the block
state) and requires chained blocks to recover.
• We evaluate LogSig-SSM on four benchmarks: long-sequence time-series classification
(UEA), high-frequency physiological signal regression (PPG-DaLiA), multivariate weather
forecasting, and irregularly sampled early sepsis prediction (PhysioNet Sepsis). LogSig-
SSM matches or exceeds strong SSM and continuous-time baselines and reduces training
time by 30× and GPU memory by 37× relative to standard Mamba on the longest sequences.

2 Theoretical Background
We review the theoretical foundations of LogSig-SSM: (i) structured SSMs, which serve as the
sequential processing backbone, (ii) their continuous-time interpretation as linear CDEs, and (iii)
log-signatures, which provide compact summaries of multivariate time-series over time intervals.
2.1 Structured State-Space Models
Modern SSM sequence layers can be viewed as discretisations of a continuous-time linear state-space
system. Let X : [0, 1] → Rd be an input signal observed at time points 0 ≤ t0 < · · · < tL ≤ 1.

produces a scalar output yi : [0, 1] → R:

3 LogSig-SSM Methodology
We introduce LogSig-SSM, a selective SSM driven by log-signature tokenisation. LogSig-SSM (i)
compresses a multivariate input sequence into a sequence of interval descriptors using multi-scale
windowed log-signatures, and (ii) processes the resulting token stream with a selective SSM backbone
for efficient long-range propagation. Figure 1 summarises the discrete-time implementation.

4 Theoretical Analysis
We highlight three theoretical attributes of LogSig-SSM. First, we establish a depth-truncation tradeoff
by showing that injecting cross-channel path geometry directly into the tokens allows LogSig-SSM to
expose non-commutative multivariate features in a single diagonal block that would otherwise require
deep, chained Mamba blocks [7]. Second, tokenisation reduces the effective sequence length, cutting
selective scan complexity by a factor of T /T ′ relative to Mamba. Third, LogSig-SSM inherits within-
window time-reparameterisation invariance from the log-signature transform, providing robustness to
irregular sampling.
4.1 Single-layer expressivity via a log-signature driver lift
A single diagonal Mamba block cannot represent non-commutative cross-channel iterated integrals
of its input path; recovering them requires chained Mamba blocks (Cor. 4.4 and Sec. C.2.1 of Cirone
et al. [7]). LogSig-SSM bypasses this depth cost by lifting the input before the scan. By construction,
the token Uτ contains every log-signature coordinate of the windowed path Wk(X)τ up to depth mk.
The following proposition formalises the resulting depth–truncation tradeoff.
Proposition 4.1 (Depth–truncation tradeoff; Proof in App. A.3). Fix a compact family X ⊂
C 1 b ([0, Tend]; Rdx ) with dx ≥ 2, a window scale (Lk, mk), an anchor τ , and 2 ≤ m ≤ mk.
Let F : X → R be a continuous functional that factors as F (X) = f (LogSigm(Wk(X)τ )) for
some continuous f . Then: (i) for every ε > 0, a single LogSig-SSM block followed by a post-scan
MLP approximates F uniformly on X to error ε; (ii) there exist such F with F /∈ F (1) Mamba[X ], where
F (1) Mamba[X ] denotes the uniform closure on X of functionals computable by a single diagonal Mamba block on the raw input path (Cor. 4.4 of [7]); (iii) any raw-input Mamba approximation of such F
requires chained diagonal blocks; recovering depth-m cross-channel features in particular requires
chains of depth growing with m in the construction of Cirone et al. [7] (Sec. C.2.1, Prop. 4.5),
whereas LogSig-SSM realises F in a single block whenever mk ≥ m.
Remark 4.2 (Multi-scale and asymptotic universality). Proposition 4.1 reflects a depth–truncation
tradeoff, not an expressivity gap. The benefit is that LogSig-SSM moves the cost of cross-channel
geometry from the online selective scan to an offline, parallel tokeniser. Across scales, any depth-
≤ mk coordinate at scale k enters Uτ linearly; thus, a single LogSig-SSM block captures features that
would otherwise require a Mamba chain whose depth grows with maxk mk. By signature universality
[23], in the limit mk → ∞ a single LogSig-SSM block approximates any continuous functional of
windowed paths, consistent with the asymptotic universality of chained diagonal Mamba on raw X
(Prop. 4.5 of Cirone et al. [7]).
4.2 Computational Efficiency and Scan Complexity
The selective scan scales linearly with sequence length and constitutes the main time-sequential
component of a Mamba layer [14]. Recent studies indicate that Mamba-style models can degrade
6
at extreme context lengths, which has been linked to length-dependent state decay and a reduced
effective memory horizon [3, 37, 40]. Consequently, reducing the effective sequence length is a
critical design component for selective SSMs. LogSig-SSM performs this compression explicitly by
replacing the length-T input sequence with a token sequence of length T ′ ≈ T /s, where s ∈ N is the
stride. Since the selective scan cost scales as O(Bsz · T · N · D), compression from T to T ′ reduces
scan FLOPs by a factor of T /T ′ ≈ s. Additionally, log-signature tokenisation is a one-time offline
pre-processing step: precomputed features are not in the training-time backpropagation graph, and
computation is highly optimised in libraries such as iisignature [29] and Signatory [18].
4.3 Robustness to Irregular Sampling
Since log-signatures are invariant under monotone time reparameterisations (Section 2.3), tokens
computed from windowed paths encode path geometry rather than sampling density. Under a
fixed interpolation, two observation sequences whose windowed paths coincide up to a monotone
reparameterisation yield identical tokens and therefore identical model outputs.
Proposition 4.3 (Within-window invariance to monotone time reparameterisation). Let bX, bX ′ :
[0, Tend] → Rdx be two bounded-variation interpolants and Iτ,k := [˜tτ − Lk, ˜tτ ] the time interval
for window (τ, k). For every scale k and anchor τ , assume there exists a continuous, non-decreasing,
surjective map γτ,k : Iτ,k → Iτ,k such that Wk( bX ′)τ (t) = Wk( bX)τ (γτ,k(t)), ∀t ∈ Iτ,k. Then
Uτ ( bX) = Uτ ( bX ′) for all τ, and LogSigSSM( bX) = LogSigSSM( bX ′).
Proof. The reparameterisation invariance of the signature (cf. (7)) gives Sig(Wk( bX)τ ) =
Sig(Wk( bX ′)τ ). Taking the truncated log-signature and applying the fixed coordinate map ϕmk
yields uτ,k( bX) = uτ,k( bX ′). Concatenating across scales gives Uτ ( bX) = Uτ ( bX ′), and the claim
follows from the determinism of the selective scan.
The theoretical guarantee is local to each window. However, Section 5.4 provides empirical evidence
that this local invariance is sufficient in practice: LogSig-SSM is highly competitive with specialised
irregular-time methods on PhysioNet Sepsis, a dataset where only 10.3% of values are observed.
5 Experimental Evaluation
We empirically test the performance of LogSig-SSM across four settings: long-range multivariate
classification, high-frequency physiological regression, multivariate weather forecasting, and irregu-
larly sampled clinical prediction. Baselines span SSM/recurrent models, continuous-time models,
and general time-series methods. We follow the Log-NCDE protocol [38] for UEA and PPG-DaLiA,
the LinOSS protocol [31] for weather forecasting, and the Neural CDE protocol [19] for PhysioNet
Sepsis. All experiments were done on a NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. Full runtime, memory, and
hyperparameter details are in Appendix B and F.
5.1 UEA time-series classification
Following the Log-NCDE protocol [38], we evaluate on the six longest-sequence datasets of the UEA
Archive [2], split 70:15:15 over five random seeds. LogSig-SSM achieves the best average accuracy
across the sixteen models (71.7%) and the best average rank (2.7) (Table 1).
LogSig-SSM demonstrates a distinct advantage on signal types where standard diagonal SSMs
severely struggle, most notably on the ETHANOLCONCENTRATION task (4 classes; chance 25%).
While diagonal SSMs fail to surpass 30% accuracy and the best non-SSM baseline reaches 40.5%,
LogSig-SSM reaches the highest accuracy of 51.4%. This signal is an absorption curve whose classes
differ by smooth shifts in peak shape over extended regions rather than by high-frequency variation
(Figure 2), a global trajectory geometry that multi-scale log-signature tokens capture directly and that
diagonal SSM backbones cannot resolve on their own.
Beyond accuracy, LogSig-SSM delivers large efficiency gains, summarised for EIGENWORMS in
Table 2. On EIGENWORMS (17,984 time points), LogSig-SSM trains in 4 s per 1000 steps versus
122 s for Mamba (30× faster), while using only 362 MB GPU memory versus 13,486 MB for Mamba
(37× less). Log-signature tokenisation is a one-time offline step taking 1.6 s for the full dataset, and
6 s for all six UEA datasets together, about 1% of a single training run per dataset (Table 6). These
7
gains come at no accuracy cost: LogSig-SSM leads on EIGENWORMS while requiring 10× less
memory than LinOSS-IM.

5.2 PPG-DaLiA time-series regression
PPG-DALIA [28] is a multimodal physiological regression benchmark for heart-rate estimation
from wrist-worn sensors. It contains recordings from 15 subjects during daily activities, each with
approximately 150 minutes of data sampled at up to 128 Hz. Following the Log-NCDE protocol [38],
recordings are converted into long sequences via a sliding window of length 49,920 and stride 4,992.
LogSig-SSM attains MSE 4.46×10−2, a 28% improvement over D-LinOSS [4] (6.16×10−2) and
58% over Mamba [14] (10.65×10−2), the strongest relative gain across all four benchmarks (Table 3,
left). On these ultra-long sequences (49,920 time steps), log-signature tokenisation directly alleviates
the long-context degradation that limits standard selective SSMs, making training feasible in regimes
where several baselines are memory-constrained.
5.3 Weather Forecasting
Following the precedent set by prior state-space models [15, 31], we evaluate LogSig-SSM’s capacity
to serve as a general-purpose sequence-to-sequence model on long-horizon forecasting tasks. We
focus on the challenging weather prediction benchmark introduced by Zhou et al. [42], specifically
targeting the hardest configuration: predicting the future 720 timesteps (one month of hourly multi-
variate climatological data) based directly on the preceding 720 timesteps. Without incorporating
any domain-specific inductive biases or specialised forecasting architectures, LogSig-SSM achieves
an MAE of 0.432 (Table 3), reducing the best prior result from D-LinOSS (0.486) by 11% and
outperforming all other baselines by a larger margin. These results demonstrate that multi-scale
log-signature tokenisation effectively generalises to standard long-horizon forecasting settings.

5.4 Sepsis Prediction on PhysioNet (Irregular Sampling)
We evaluate on the PhysioNet 2019 early sepsis prediction challenge [30, 12]. Comprising 40,335
variable-length ICU stays with 34 features, this dataset is extremely sparse (only 10.3% observed
values), providing a direct test of the irregular-sampling robustness established in Section 4.3.
Predicting sepsis occurrence based on the first 72 hours, we evaluate AUROC both with and without
appended observation indicators (OI) (Table 3, right).
LogSig-SSM achieves an AUROC of 90.4/87.4 (with/without OI). It performs competitively with
purpose-built irregular-time models (Neural LSDE, LNSDE, GSDE [26]) and outperforms Neural

6 Conclusion
We introduce LogSig-SSM, which combines multi-scale windowed log-signature tokenisation with
a selective SSM backbone for long-horizon time-series modelling. By replacing pointwise inputs
with interval descriptors, LogSig-SSM compresses long, irregularly sampled sequences into a shorter
token stream while injecting higher-order cross-channel information through log-signature features,
enabling scan-efficient long-range propagation while increasing single-block multivariate expressivity.
Beyond its discrete-time formulation, LogSig-SSM extends the linear CDE view of selective SSMs [7]
to log-signature-derived driving paths, yielding an NRDE-style interval-driven form in which se-
lectivity allocates internal time across the compressed sequence in a content-dependent manner.
Empirically, LogSig-SSM achieves the best or competitive performance across the four benchmarks
above while substantially reducing training time and GPU memory relative to Mamba-style baselines.
Ablation studies confirm that the gains arise from the geometric content of log-signature tokens, with
selective recurrence providing additional margin on tasks requiring content-dependent aggregation.
A limitation for LogSig-SSM is that for genuinely single-channel paths, log-signature tokens reduce
to path increments and the higher-order cross-channel structure becomes degenerate, although
augmenting the path with time as an additional channel restores non-trivial structure. The token width
grows as O(dm x /m), so tokenisation time and the size of the input embedding increase rapidly with
the number of channels. Finally, window lengths and stride are still selected per dataset (Appendix F).
Learned or adaptive window selection is a natural direction for reducing this overhead.

Table 5 summarises the compute requirements of each model on the UEA classification benchmarks

LogSig-SSM is the most compute-efficient approach. It has the lowest memory usage on all six
datasets, often an order of magnitude below Mamba, and it is the fastest (or joint fastest) model on
all six datasets, with large gains over the continuous-time baselines. These gains follow from the
architecture: log-signature tokenisation shortens the effective sequence before the selective scan,
reducing both the number of recurrent updates and the activations stored for backpropagation. On the
two widest datasets (Heartbeat, 61 channels; MotorImagery, 64 channels) the depth-two tokens are
1,891- and 4,160-dimensional, which raises the parameter count of the input embedding but leaves
the footprint at 54 MB and 78 MB.
Tokenisation is a one-time offline computation and is not part of the training graph. Table 6 reports
its cost with iisignature [29] on CPU, with the Lie basis built once per dataset rather than once
per series (the emitted features are identical): all six datasets are tokenised in 6 s in total, about 1% of
one training run per dataset. Tokenisation is linear in the sequence length and in the number of series,
parallel over both, and the work at each anchor is set by maxk Lk and maxk mk rather than by the
length of the stream, so it also runs online: tokens are emitted every s samples with latency bounded
by s and a buffer of maxk Lk samples, and the optional global branch can be maintained at constant
cost per anchor through Chen’s identity. The binding constraint is channel width, since the depth-m
token has O(dm x /m) coordinates.

C.1 Signature versus log-signature tokens
The truncated signature and log-signature are related by a polynomial bijection, so at equal depth they
carry identical information and any continuous functional of one is a continuous functional of the
other. What the log-signature gives up is universality for linear readouts, which the nonlinear readout
of LogSig-SSM does not need. We use the log-signature because it is the driver with which the
models we build on are defined (the log-ODE method underlying NRDEs [25] and Log-NCDEs [38]),
because it is the redundancy-free coordinate system for that content, and because iisignature
accumulates it directly in a Lie basis rather than taking a logarithm of the signature. Table 8 compares
the two token types directly in accuracy and tokenisation time on EigenWorms, SCP1 and SCP2, the
three datasets on which the second-order terms are load-bearing (Appendix D): the log-signature
coordinates of the reported configuration are replaced by signature coordinates of the same depth with
every other hyperparameter fixed, and since the two coincide at depth one the comparison isolates
how the higher levels are coordinatised. The log-signature leads on all three, by 3.1 points on average,
and is about twice as fast to compute.

E Signal Visualisation
We visualise representative signals from the six UEA classification tasks used in our evaluation
(Figure 2). The goal is to highlight the qualitative differences in temporal structure across datasets,
which helps explain the heterogeneous empirical performance observed in Table 1.
The datasets separate naturally into two regimes. Five datasets (SelfRegulationSCP1, SelfRegu-
lationSCP2, MotorImagery, Heartbeat, EigenWorms) exhibit high-frequency, oscillatory, or noise-
dominated dynamics, where discriminative information is often localised in short-time patterns or
frequency content. In contrast, EthanolConcentration consists of smooth absorption spectra with a
single broad peak, where class differences manifest as global shifts in shape and location over long
time horizons.
This distinction is important for interpreting our results. Multi-scale log-signature tokens sum-
marise the geometry of the path over extended intervals, capturing global shape characteristics and
cross-channel relationships. This is well matched to signals such as EthanolConcentration, where
discriminative structure is inherently low-frequency and global. This supports the interpretation that
the gains of LogSig-SSM arise from its ability to capture multi-scale path geometry, rather than
compression alone.

F.1 Hyperparameter optimisation protocol
We optimise log-signature tokenisation and the Mamba backbone using a two-stage Optuna [1]
protocol with 100 trials per stage. Stage 1 searches the tokenisation space with the backbone fixed
at a small reference Mamba (d=64, 2 layers, N =32, convolution width 3, expansion 2). Stage
2 searches the Mamba backbone jointly with a local neighbourhood of the stage-1 tokenisation.
The same protocol and budget are used for the LogSig-S5 and LogSig-Transformer ablations, with
backbone-appropriate hyperparameter ranges substituted in Stage 2.

Comparison to prior protocols. For fairness with prior work, we adopt the same Mamba hyperpa-
rameter grid as the Log-NCDE protocol [38] and its models following this protocol [31, 4]: learning

same space as the strongest published Mamba results on these benchmarks, so the comparison in
Table 1 is structurally fair. Second, our budget is smaller in raw trial count but is allocated adaptively
rather than uniformly. Stage 1 separately searches the tokenisation space, so the total budget per
dataset is 200 trials, of which 100 involve backbone selection.
