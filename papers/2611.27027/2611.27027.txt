collisional–radiative plasma kinetics

Abstract
Collisional–radiative (CR) models govern the non-equilibrium population kinetics of par-
tially ionized plasmas and are a dominant cost in integrated edge- and divertor-plasma sim-
ulations, where a stiff system of dozens to thousands of coupled rate equations must be solved
at every spatial cell and time step. We present a two-stage machine learning (ML) surrogate
that replaces the direct CR solve with a compact, continuous-time, deployable dynamical
model. Stage 1 is a grey-box autoencoder that compresses a high-dimensional ion population
vector into a low-dimensional latent space in which four coordinates, one per charge state of
the lithium system studied here, are fixed to the physical charge-state distribution and the
rest are learned. Stage 2 is a latent neural ODE that learns the continuous-time evolution
of the latent variables. We (i) integrate in a density-folded, logarithmic clock on which the
latent trajectory is smooth enough for an explicit integrator, (ii) sample time adaptively by
latent arc length so the sharp ionization transition is resolved, (iii) add a linear damping
toward an estimate of the CR steady state as a design choice, and (iv) enforce mass con-
servation directly. The surrogate is accurate, with a charge-state error that is unchanged
under refinement of its output grid, over full-trajectory rollouts within the trained horizon,
and exactly conservative: on 1480 held-out trajectories at the trained conditions it repro-
duces the charge-state evolution with a median absolute error of 4.2 × 10−3 on fractions in
[0, 1], conserves mass to 10−7, and evaluates a full sixteen-decade trajectory in ∼76 µs per
trajectory in batched inference on a single GPU. A model retrained with whole (nA, Te) grid
points withheld is equally accurate at those unseen conditions (median 3.8 × 10−3).
Keywords: collisional–radiative model; stiff kinetics; neural ODE; surrogate model
1 Introduction
In a partially ionized plasma that is neither in local thermodynamic equilibrium nor in coronal
equilibrium, the populations of atomic and ionic energy levels are governed by the compe-
tition between collisional processes (electron-impact excitation, de-excitation, and ionization,
and collisional (three-body) recombination), radiative processes (spontaneous emission, radia-
tive recombination), and dielectronic recombination, a resonant capture stabilized by photon
emission [4, 5]. The collisional–radiative (CR) model [2] encodes this competition as a system
of coupled ordinary differential equations for the level population vector N (t) ∈ Rn,

impact processes, radiative and dielectronic recombination) are quadratic in N , and collisional
recombination, which scales as n2 e , is cubic; at fixed conditions (1) is therefore autonomous but
polynomially nonlinear in N ; every trajectory nonetheless relaxes to a steady state, referred
to below as the CR steady state (Section 3.3). The dimension n ranges from tens to many
thousands of levels depending on the resolution (superconfiguration, configuration-averaged,
or fine-structure) [28]; the lithium model treated in this paper resolves n = 94 levels across
four charge states. CR models generalize the equilibrium ionization balance long tabulated for
astrophysical plasmas [1] to finite density and to time-dependent conditions, and they supply the
line emission and radiative power loss that close the equations for impurity-transport and plasma
energy evolution in magnetic-confinement fusion; they are likewise central to spectroscopic
diagnostics and to opacity and cooling-curve calculations [8].
Equation (1) is severely stiff : the real parts of the eigenvalues of the instantaneous M differ
by many orders of magnitude, because collisional and radiative rates differ greatly in size and
scale differently with nA and Te. Stiffness of this kind is characteristic of CR and also of chemical
kinetics in general, and is the primary obstacle to building learned surrogates for such systems
[15, 18, 14, 21]. Resolving the fast modes requires implicit integration or small time steps, and
because the system is nonlinear each implicit step is a Newton iteration, every pass of which
solves a linear system in the level populations, an O(n3) factorization repeated several times per
step for n up to thousands of levels. When a CR solve is embedded in an integrated transport
code, such as SOLPS-ITER [31], or fluid/gyrokinetic edge codes, it must be evaluated at every
spatial cell and time step across a wide range of local (nA, Te) conditions, so the aggregate
CR cost can dominate the simulation. This motivates fast surrogates that reproduce the CR
dynamics at a small fraction of the cost while remaining accurate and physically consistent
enough to be trusted inside a multi-physics simulation. We will demonstrate our surrogate
modeling approach with the case study of lithium, a low-Z element of growing interest as a
liquid plasma-facing material, whose CR kinetics govern its ionization and radiation in the edge
plasma [9].
Deep learning has recently advanced core fusion tasks from disruption prediction to plasma
control [17, 10], and physics-informed learning provides a general framework for embedding
physical structure in such models [16]. For CR kinetics specifically, machine-learning (ML)
surrogates are attractive because the CR solution manifold is low-dimensional in practice: in
this work, the 94 levels of a single lithium species compress to a ten-dimensional latent space
(Section 3.3). Neural-network surrogates for CR models have been developed with adaptive
sampling of the parameter space [12, 13]. A prior two-stage approach [32] exploited this structure
directly: a physics-assisted autoencoder compresses the population vector into a compact latent
variable, which a discrete-time flow-map network (FMNet) then advances by a fixed step. That
work established the viability of the latent-surrogate idea but left three limitations that matter
for deployment: (i) the flow map is tied to the training time step, so the surrogate is not
readily usable at the variable steps an integrated code takes; (ii) errors accumulate over long
rollouts; and (iii) mass conservation is imposed only softly, through a loss penalty, so the
predicted populations may not sum to unity. The surrogate developed here addresses all three:
it is continuous in time, so the output times along a trajectory are a free choice rather than
a property fixed at training (advancing an arbitrary intermediate state is a separate matter,
discussed under Limitations), its integrated field includes a linear damping toward an estimate
of the CR steady state, and it conserves mass exactly by construction. A full trajectory takes
∼76 µs on a single GPU when trajectories are evaluated in batches (Section 4.1), a throughput
compatible with in-loop use.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 places the surrogate in the
context of learned reduced models for stiff kinetics, neural differential equations and their stabi-
lization, and the exact enforcement of physical constraints. Section 3 states the problem, devel-
ops the grey-box autoencoder and the latent neural ODE, and then characterizes the structural
properties of the data that each design choice exploits. Section 4 evaluates the surrogate on
held-out trajectories and against the retrained flow-map baseline, and Section 5 concludes.
2 Related Work
Our surrogate sits at the intersection of three lines of work: learned reduced models for stiff
kinetics, neural differential equations, and structure-preserving ML with strongly enforced phys-
ical constraints; we close by contrasting the latent-dynamics route with operator learning. Surro-
gates for stiff chemical kinetics have been built as neural ODEs [22] and as physics-informed neu-
ral networks [15], while reduced models of stiff dynamics more generally combine autoencoder-
based coordinate reduction [6] with discrete-time flow maps or direct state-to-state regression.
Relative to the CR surrogate of Xie et al. [32] described in Section 1, we keep the grey-box au-
toencoder and replace only the flow map. Recent high-fidelity CR modeling for fusion-relevant
species [27, 26] would supply the trajectory ensembles needed to extend surrogates of this type
beyond lithium.
The dynamical core builds on neural ODEs, which parametrize the right-hand side of an
ODE with a neural network and optimize it by differentiating the solve, either directly or through
a time-reverse adjoint system [7], and on the latent-ODE variant that learns the dynamics in a
learned latent space and is well suited to irregularly sampled series [25]. Training such models
on stiff systems is challenging, because both the forward and adjoint solves inherit the stiffness
[18]; the principal remedies are equation scaling and time reparametrization, which transform
the integration variable so the fastest mode becomes O(1) [3], and architectural or gradient
regularization that improves conditioning [11]. Our density-folded logarithmic clock is a physics-
defined reparametrization in this family; we show that, combined with the latent reduction and
arc-length sampling, it permits an explicit Runge–Kutta solver to integrate the lithium system
accurately in about a hundred steps per trajectory (Section 4.1). A separate difficulty is that
long autonomous rollouts of learned dynamics tend to drift or diverge. A now-standard fix adds
a linear damping term whose fixed point anchors the long-time solution. Linot et al. [19] learn
such a linear operator jointly with the nonlinear vector field and show it keeps trajectories on
the attractor for chaotic and shock-laden systems. We adopt this damping term but specialize
it to our setting: rather than letting the spectrum of a learned operator define the attractor,
3
we damp toward an estimate of the CR steady state, regressed on (nA, Te) from the terminal
states of the simulated trajectories. In our trained model the damping does not make the
terminal state attracting, and a model trained without it is as accurate (Sections 4.5 and 4.6),
so here it is a design choice rather than a demonstrated stabilizer. Closely related in spirit,
physics-informed latent dynamics identification with stability and steady-state constraints has
recently been applied to time-dependent NLTE atomic kinetics [20], sharing our premise that
atomic-kinetics surrogates should model the time evolution and be anchored to the correct
equilibrium.
We also draw on work that enforces conservation laws exactly rather than through soft penal-
ties. In combustion and reacting-flow networks, a projection or constraint layer can guarantee
element and mass conservation [24, 30]; we adopt an analogous simplex projection, applied to
the level-resolved output, whose partial sums then give charge-state fractions that satisfy the
constraint identically (Section 3.1). A numerically stable logarithmic conservation penalty is
also kept in both training stages, so that the learned representation and dynamics stay close to
the constraint and the projection has little to correct.
A different formulation bypasses latent dynamics altogether and learns the solution operator
mapping conditions and time directly to the state. Such operator-learning surrogates have been
demonstrated on stiff chemical kinetics [14], and a linear latent network that integrates its
latent dynamics analytically has likewise been demonstrated as a real-time solution operator
for stiff kinetics, including a lithium CR model [21]. We pursue the latent-dynamics route
because it yields an interpretable, conservable, continuous-time integrator. We do not attempt
a head-to-head comparison against these operator-learning surrogates: a meaningful one would
require running them on identical trajectory data, and no common benchmark yet exists for CR
kinetics. Our quantitative baseline is instead the discrete flow-map surrogate of Xie et al. [32],
retrained here on the identical data, split, and latent representation (Section 4), which isolates
the effect of the continuous-time formulation.
3 Proposed Approach
This work focuses on the lithium CR system of (1), for which we follow the notation of Xie et al.
[32]. The state N (t) ∈ R94 is the population density of lithium, in m−3, over the ground and
excited states of the four charge states n0 (neutral) through n3 (fully stripped). The background
is a Maxwellian electron distribution of temperature Te (in eV) together with the total lithium
density nA = ∑ i Ni; quasineutrality sets the electron density from the instantaneous charge-
state densities, so M depends on the state as well as on (nA, Te), which fix the background.
Because N spans many decades across conditions we normalize the density populations ~N =
N/nA, whose group sums over the levels of each charge state give the charge-state fractions
nz ∈ R4, with ∑ i nz,i = 1 by construction. The remaining output is the radiative power loss
RL(t), the rate at which the electrons lose energy to radiation. Given (nA, Te) and an initial
state, the surrogate must reproduce N (t) and, from it, the nz (t) and RL(t) that downstream
transport and radiation models consume.
We describe the two stages in turn, the grey-box autoencoder that learns the latent rep-
resentation (Section 3.1) and the latent neural ODE that advances it (Section 3.2), and then
characterize in Section 3.3 the properties of the data that each design choice exploits.

Why a learned latent field. The latent field is learned from trajectories, not derived from
(1). No derivative labels are supplied: the network predicts a field, the field is integrated, and
the parameters are adjusted to reduce the error of the integrated trajectory against the encoded
CR states at the saved times. The alternative is to transform the physical equations through
the encoder: if L′ = E(N ) denotes the complete encoding, the chain rule gives

to remove, and it is exact only if physically distinct states with the same encoding have the
same encoded derivative, a closure that no fixed compression guarantees. We therefore learn
the field directly and treat it as a predictive model over the sampled distribution of trajectories:
accurate held-out rollouts support that use, but do not identify the field uniquely away from
the data or establish reliable evolution from arbitrary latent states (Section 5).
Time reparametrization. The model is a parametrized family of dynamics conditioned on
c in (5). It integrates in a density-folded logarithmic clock τ rather than in physical time, for
two reasons. The kinetics are stiff in t, spanning sixteen decades with the charge-state rate
per second varying by roughly eight orders of magnitude along one trajectory, but smooth per
decade of log10 t: a logarithmic clock stretches those decades into a unit interval on which the
latent trajectory is smooth apart from one localized transition, so that a fixed budget of about
a hundred steps can be placed where the state moves. The transition also moves with density,
ttrans ∝ n−1 A , so a plain log10 t clock would place it somewhere different for every density and the
vector field would have to learn that shift. Multiplying time by nA before taking the logarithm
holds t nA at the transition roughly constant, which brings the transitions much closer together
on the clock. Normalizing to the unit interval,

do not by themselves guarantee resolution of the narrowest transient. In the single-run ablation

loss about twelve times higher, and some of its test rollouts were not finite.

design choice, not a demonstrated requirement: in the ablation of Section 4.5, a model trained
without it (A = 0) has the same median accuracy.
Integration. Each trajectory is integrated on its non-uniform grid {τ0 < · · · < τM } (M +1 =
nτ ) by the classical explicit fourth-order Runge–Kutta scheme with steps hn = τn+1 − τn.
Training rolls out H < M steps at a time; inference integrates the whole grid. Parameter
gradients are obtained by backpropagating directly through the RK4 solve rather than by the
continuous adjoint method; with nτ = 100 the unrolled graph fits comfortably in memory, and
this avoids the second, time-reversed solve that would itself inherit any residual stiffness. Two
clarifications about what this integration does and does not establish. First, the clock does not
remove stiffness by itself: by Equation (13) it rescales the physical Jacobian by α(τ ) and leaves
eigenvalue ratios unchanged. What the explicit scheme integrates is the learned latent field,
whose Jacobian is not that of (1); the ten-dimensional latent representation does not carry the
fast physical modes, the clock spreads the slow evolution over a unit interval, and the adaptive
grid resolves the one sharp feature. The combined latent reduction, time reparametrization,
and sampling strategy permits accurate explicit integration on the tested trajectories; we do
not claim elimination of the original fast modes, and this paper does not separate the three
contributions by ablation (the ablation of Section 4.5 changes the training grid only) or examine
9
the Jacobian of the complete learned field, so no one of them is credited with the result. Second,
the arc-length grid is an output-sampling choice, not an error or stability control: each pair
of adjacent grid points is one RK4 step, with no internal substepping or error estimate, and
the state-speed criterion of Equation (14) says nothing about the stability bound hλ ≲ 2.8 of
classical RK4 for a mode of rate λ. Stability and accuracy are therefore verified empirically
by grid refinement (Section 4.4): the charge-state error is unchanged from 100 to 400 points
(the latent error on the late plateau is not, see Figure 12), and a 50-point grid is unstable,
which locates the stability boundary of the learned field on this family of grids. Section 4.5 also
integrates the same field with an error-controlled scheme and measures the RK4 integration
error directly. Larger steps than those trained, and extrapolation beyond the clock domain, are
not justified.

Each expectation E in Equation (17) is a plain mean over every scalar entry it contains: over
the windows in the batch, the H target points of each window (the initial point is excluded),

about four hours on one NVIDIA A100 GPU for 2760 epochs, about five seconds per epoch.
On the anchor network. The steady-state estimate enters the damping through a small
anchor network Leq(c) = MLPeq(c), trained against the measured terminal latent variables
through Leq; the network, rather than the measured terminal latent variables themselves, is
used throughout training and inference, because the measured terminal states exist only for the
training trajectories whereas the network supplies a smooth Leq(c) over the condition space.
The six black coordinates of Leq have no counterpart in a charge-state balance and are known
only through the encoder applied to simulated terminal states, and where the terminal state at
fixed c depends on the initial condition the regression returns an average over initial conditions.
Because the damping is a soft pull whose per-coordinate strength is learned, the rollouts stay
10
accurate even when Leq is only approximate; the supporting training diagnostics are reported
in Section 4.1. We call Leq an equilibrium anchor rather than an attractor because attraction
is not established. The linear form of the damping is prescribed, and both A and Leq are
fitted through the trajectory loss, not identified physical relaxation rates. At the anchor the
integrated field equals F raw θ (Leq, τ ; c), which is not constrained to vanish, and the Jacobian of
the integrated field is ∂L′ F raw θ − diag(A), whose stability positive A alone does not guarantee;
the nonlinear network can also compensate for changes in damping strength or anchor location,
so the decomposition is not uniquely identified by trajectory data. What the results show is
that rollouts reach the observed terminal states at the end of the trained clock interval over
the tested set (Section 4.4). The checks of Section 4.6 show more: the field does not vanish
at the terminal state or at the anchor, its Jacobian there has an eigenvalue with positive real
part on almost every test trajectory, and rollouts continued past the clock interval leave the
terminal state. The anchor is therefore not a stable fixed point of the learned field, and, with
the no-damping ablation of Section 4.5, the damping is not what brings the rollouts to the
terminal state.
3.3 Dataset and structural properties
The dataset comprises 14,800 trajectories on a grid of 10 background densities nA, 37 electron
temperatures Te, and 40 initial conditions. At each (nA, Te) the 40 initial conditions lie on a
straight line in population space between two extreme members (an affine fit leaves residuals of
order 10−16), and each is labelled by a scalar nper stored with the trajectory, which takes the
same 40 values at every (nA, Te); we use nper to identify initial conditions in the figures and in
the error breakdowns. Each trajectory is obtained by integrating (1) from t = 10−16 to 1 s and
records N (t), together with the four charge-state partial sums nz (group sums of the density-
normalized populations ~N = N/nA), the radiative power loss RL(t), and the conditions c. The
solution is saved at 1019 times, 1000 logarithmically spaced up to 0.1 s followed by 19 uniformly
spaced up to 1 s; we use the logarithmically spaced part throughout. These are recorded output
times of the implicit reference solver, which takes its own error-controlled internal steps between
them; the arc-length grid of Section 3.2 selects among the recorded output times and never
sees the solver’s internal steps. The data are split by trajectory into train/validation/test
(80/10/10) at random. Because each (nA, Te) grid point carries 40 trajectories, every grid point is
represented in the training set, and a test trajectory shares its conditions with about 32 training
trajectories that differ in the initial condition. The test set therefore measures generalization
across initial conditions at the trained conditions; generalization to unseen (nA, Te) is tested
separately, with a split that holds out whole (nA, Te) grid points, in Section 4.5.
Each design choice above exploits a measurable property of these trajectories. We quantify
those properties here over the full set (Figure 3) and, for each property, name the design decision
it supports. They fall into two groups: those that govern the treatment of time, and those that
concern the structure of the state space.
The kinetics are stiff in physical time but the sampled trajectories are smooth in logarithmic
time. On the saved grid the step size ranges over ∆tmax/∆tmin ≈ 1.3 × 1016, and the ionization
transition unfolds between 10−8 and 10−6 s within a 1 s horizon. That fast feature is six to
eight orders of magnitude narrower than the integration interval, and the fast modes of (1)
are faster still, which is why an implicit solver was used to generate the data. Measured per
decade of log10 t rather than per second, the peak charge-state rate drops from about 108 to
about 10 (Figure 3a), and the transition spreads over a median 17% of the clock range. These
are properties of the sampled trajectory, not of the stability spectrum: the clock leaves the
eigenvalue ratios of (1) unchanged (Equation (13)). What they show is that on the clock the
latent path is a slow evolution with one localized feature, so that a modest number of steps
placed by the arc-length sampling below can follow it, which is what the explicit integration of
the learned field exploits (Section 3.2).

The transition also moves with density. The time at which the neutral fraction drops below
one half (the first recorded time with n0 < 1/2, which defines ttrans throughout; every trajectory
in the dataset crosses, so none is excluded) spans 2.3 decades across the dataset and scales as
n−1.08 A , with negligible residual dependence on Te (Figure 3b). Folding nA into the clock removes
the density trend and halves the spread of transition locations, from a standard deviation of
0.026 to 0.014 of the clock range (Figure 3c, Section 3.2). The clock alone does not widen the
transition enough: for the sharpest trajectories it still spans only 0.3% of the τ range. On a
100-point grid, uniform sampling puts a median of 17 points inside the transition and none at
all for the sharpest, whereas arc-length sampling puts a median of 32 there and never fewer
than 5 (Section 3.2).
Three properties of the state space shape the representation. First, the charge fractions are
approximately stationary by the end of almost every trajectory. The charge-state drift over
the final 50 samples has median 2 × 10−8, and the dominant terminal fractions are set by the
conditions: across the dataset they move from singly ionized at low Te to fully stripped at high
Te, with a smooth crossover. At fixed conditions, trace populations such as the post-transition
n0 settle to values that depend on the initial condition (Figure 7) and remain constant to 1 s.
The target of the damping term can therefore be estimated from the conditions only up to
12
that spread (Section 3.2). The spread of the drift is wide: over all 14,800 trajectories its 95th
percentile is 2.7 × 10−5, and 2% of the trajectories drift by more than 10−3, all at Te ≤ 10 eV
and nA ≤ 5×1014; the largest, 9.2×10−2, occurs at nA = 1014 and Te = 7.5 eV, where the charge
fractions are still evolving at the final saved time. At Te ≥ 20 eV the drift is below 2.2 × 10−6
for every trajectory. Approximate stationarity of the four charge fractions therefore holds at
the end of most, not all, trajectories, and it does not establish convergence of every level or
latent coordinate, uniqueness of the terminal state, or attraction toward it; the accuracy of the
surrogate at the final time is reported in Section 4.5. Second, the populations compress well.
Principal components of the log-populations capture 93% of the variance in four, 98% in six
and 99.4% in ten (Figure 3d), so ten latent coordinates, four fixed to the charge states and
six learned, retain essentially all resolvable variance (Section 3.1). Captured variance supports
compressibility; whether the 4+6 split is sufficient, and whether the low-variance channels are
dynamically relevant, is established only by the reconstruction and rollout errors of Section 4.
Third, the radiative power loss is smooth in the populations. Its logarithm is approximately
linear in the 94 log-populations (R2 ≈ 0.97) over roughly five decades, so it decodes from the
same latent variable under the standardization of Section 3.1.
4 Numerical Results
We evaluate the surrogate (the frozen grey-box autoencoder of Section 3.1 composed with the
latent neural ODE of Section 3.2) on the held-out test trajectories. Unless stated otherwise,
every rollout is produced by integrating the latent dynamics from the true initial latent variable
only and decoding to physical charge states nz and radiative power loss RL; no ground-truth
information enters the rollout after τ0. We report the training behaviour (Section 4.1), charge-
state rollout fidelity and its distribution over the parameter space (Section 4.2), the radiative
power loss (Section 4.3), mass conservation, grid refinement, and long-rollout behaviour (Sec-
tion 4.4), deployable grids, unseen conditions, integration error, and ablations (Section 4.5), the
learned field near the terminal state, restarts, and perturbations (Section 4.6), and a controlled
comparison with the discrete flow-map surrogate (Section 4.7).

4.4 Mass conservation and long-rollout stability

and maximum 6 × 10−7 (Table 3). The predicted populations are therefore always a valid
distribution, in contrast to the soft penalty of the prior flow-map surrogate, under which the
sum could drift. This guarantee concerns the decoded sum only: it says nothing about the
white latent block, whose predicted charge fractions are a second estimate of nz that need not
coincide with the decoded partial sums, and nothing about kinetic accuracy, which the rollout

the clock range of the data, not dynamical stability: Section 4.6 shows that the terminal state
is not attracting for the learned field, that perturbations can grow transiently, and that rollouts
continued past the clock range leave the terminal state; a model trained without the damping
term is as accurate at the median (Section 4.5).

on the remaining points (median MAE 5.6, 8.2, and 5.0 × 10−3 for both), so the error is not
carried by accumulated state error. Restarting from the model’s own state at its clock value
reproduces the uninterrupted rollout exactly, since RK4 is a one-step method. Restarted from
the same true states with the clock reset to τ0, the rollout fails: the decoded output is non-finite
on 17–72% of the trajectories, depending on the restart point, and the median MAE of the rest
is 0.15 to 0.24. The field is not invariant under a shift of the clock, and a restart requires the
clock value of the trajectory. Random perturbations of norm 0.01 or 0.1 of the standardized
latent state at the same three points grow transiently, by a median factor of up to 1.4, and by
the final time have decayed to a median 0.3 to 0.55 of their initial size; the 95th percentile of
that ratio is 0.8 to 1.2, so on some trajectories they are not reduced, and the largest growth is
a factor of 26. For norm 0.1 the final decoded charge fractions change by a median of at most
2 × 10−4 and at most 0.2. No CR solution exists for the perturbed states, so this measures the
sensitivity of the learned field, not its accuracy.
4.7 Comparison with the discrete flow-map surrogate
The direct predecessor of this work [32] pairs the same grey-box autoencoder with a discrete-
time flow map that advances the latent variable by one step, Ln+1 = Φ(∆tn, Ln; θ), trained
by one-step teacher forcing on the native non-uniform step sequence, with mass conservation
imposed as a soft penalty in the loss. For a controlled comparison we retrain a flow map
of this form on the identical data: the same frozen autoencoder latent variables, the same
train/validation/test split, and a matched network capacity, with the clock supplied as simple
normalized log10 t. One-step training of the direct map is recursively unstable: without a small
train-time input-noise regularization (σ = 3 × 10−3) the 999-step rollout diverges on nearly all
validation trajectories, and even with it, training must be stopped by rollout-validated early
stopping while the one-step loss is still decreasing, so the baseline reported here is the strongest
we could construct.
Table 6 and Figure 16 summarize the outcome. On the identical native grid the continuous-
time model is an order of magnitude more accurate (median relative L2 of 1.3 × 10−2 versus
1.3 × 10−1; median MAE 1.6 × 10−3 versus 1.4 × 10−2), its worst-case trajectory is seven
times better (5.9 × 10−2 versus 4.3 × 10−1), and it exhibits none of the flow map’s outright
divergences. The per-step error profile makes the mechanism visible: the flow map’s error
compounds along the recursion, whereas the neural ODE’s error is a function of position in the
trajectory alone. Per network evaluation the two models cost about the same (the flow map

Limitations. The study is scoped to a single species (lithium) on a fixed grid of time-
independent background conditions; time-varying nA and Te, as occur along a transport-code
time step, are not addressed. This is a structural restriction of the present formulation, not only
a missing test: the clock τ depends on nA, so a time-varying density changes the clock itself,
and a time-varying Te changes the conditioning the field was trained to hold fixed. Coupling to
a transport code therefore requires either a state-defined clock or integration of the learned field
in physical time, and data with time-varying conditions to validate it. The surrogate is vali-
dated within the temporal horizon of the training data and at output resolutions at or above the
training grid; coarser grids under-resolve the transition, and beyond the trained clock range the
surrogate fails (Section 4.6) and must not be used. The anchor network is only coarsely accurate,
although this does not affect rollout accuracy within the horizon (Section 4.1); the damping
term is an inductive bias that gave no measurable accuracy gain in a single-run ablation, and
the terminal state is not a stable fixed point of the learned field (Section 4.6). Species-number
conservation of the decoded populations is the only quantity enforced exactly; energy balance,
reaction rates, and radiation are learned, not enforced, and the learned latent field is a pre-
dictive model over the sampled trajectories rather than an identified kinetic law (Section 3.2).
Three further restrictions follow from how the surrogate is built and tested. First, the main
results use a split by trajectory, at trained conditions; the held-out-condition test of Section 4.5
covers interpolation between trained grid points for one random draw, not extrapolation beyond
28
the trained range of (nA, Te). Second, the main results are integrated on arc-length grids built
from the reference trajectory; the deployable two-pass grid of Section 4.5 nearly matches them
at twice the cost, whereas grids fixed in advance fail on a few trajectories, and the 100-point
RK4 rollout is not a converged solution of the learned field on every trajectory. Third, the
clock τ is elapsed time from the common initial time t = 10−16 s, so the surrogate advances a
trajectory from its initial state but cannot be restarted from an arbitrary intermediate state,
which has no τ assigned to it. A state on a computed trajectory restarts at its own clock value
with unchanged accuracy, whereas resetting the clock fails (Section 4.6). Assigning τ from the
state itself, for example through its charge-state progress, which the folded clock may make
well defined, is a natural remedy. Several evaluations that would sharpen these statements have
not been run and are left for a later study: the constrained field α(τ )gθ(L′; c) of Section 3.2;
repetitions of the ablations of Section 4.5 over random seeds; a field whose terminal state is
attracting, for example through a constraint on its Jacobian there; states changed by transport
between steps; and validation outside the trained range of conditions.
5 Conclusions
We have presented a continuous-time surrogate for stiff collisional–radiative kinetics, built on
the grey-box latent space of Xie et al. [32] and driven by a latent neural ODE with the linear
damping of [19]. The central enabler is a physics-based time reparametrization: integrating in a
density-folded logarithmic clock maps the sixteen decades of physical time onto a unit interval
on which the latent trajectory is smooth apart from one localized transition, which arc-length-
adaptive sampling resolves, and aligns the ionization transition across the parameter grid. The
clock does not remove stiffness in the spectral sense; it is the combination of latent reduction,
reparametrization, and adaptive sampling that lets an explicit Runge–Kutta integrator replace
the implicit stiff solver on the tested trajectories, with a single small vector field (5.9 × 104
parameters) capturing the dynamics of the full ensemble. The integrated field includes a linear
damping toward an estimate of the CR steady state, regressed on (nA, Te) from the simulated
terminal states, as an inductive bias, and the rollouts reach the observed terminal states at the
end of the trained clock interval. The terminal state is not attracting for the learned field, and
a model trained without the damping is as accurate at the median, so the damping does not
produce that behaviour; the surrogate must not be integrated past the clock range of the data.
On 1480 held-out trajectories the surrogate reproduces the charge-state evolution with a
median absolute error of 4.2 × 10−3 on fractions in [0, 1], within a factor of 2.6 of the autoen-
coder’s own reconstruction baseline; conserves mass by construction, to within 4 × 10−7; has a
charge-state error that is unchanged under refinement of its output grid at or above the train-
ing resolution, with a per-step latent error that depends on position along the trajectory rather
than on the number of steps through the ionization transition, but grows with refinement on
the late plateau; and evaluates in 76 µs per full trajectory on a single GPU in batched infer-
ence. A model retrained with whole (nA, Te) grid points held out is as accurate at those unseen
conditions as at trained ones (median MAE 3.8 × 10−3 and 3.7 × 10−3), and a two-pass grid
built from the model’s own prediction replaces the reference grid at nearly the same accuracy
and 153 µs per trajectory.
The study is scoped to a single species (lithium) on a fixed condition grid, and its quantita-
tive baseline is a retrained flow-map surrogate rather than an operator-learning alternative, a
comparison that awaits a common CR benchmark. Natural next steps are a validation beyond
the trained range of (nA, Te), a state-defined clock that allows the surrogate to be restarted from
an arbitrary state, the coupling of the surrogate into an integrated transport code, where its
continuous-time formulation and hard conservation are designed to matter, and the extension to
further species and time-dependent background conditions, for which high-fidelity CR modeling
and data are now becoming available, including hybrid fine-structure and superconfiguration
29
schemes for helium, lithium, and beryllium [27] and CR data for disruption-mitigation species
[26].
