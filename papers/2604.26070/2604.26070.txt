Abstract
Causal inference in continuous-time sequential decision problems is challenged
by hidden confounding and partially observed states. We show that, under ex-
plicit structural assumptions, observability of the latent state enables identification
of dynamic treatment effects through a continuous-time conditional front-door
adjustment, even in the presence of hidden confounding.
We derive a general adjustment formula and show that it reduces to a tractable
state-space formula when unobserved contemporaneous disturbances are tempo-
rally uncorrelated. This formula expresses potential-outcome distributions under
alternative treatment trajectories through the measurement model, latent dynamics,
and the filtering distribution over latent states.
We propose Observable Neural ODEs (ObsNODEs), Neural ODE models in observ-
able normal form that implement this tractable adjustment for causal forecasting.
ObsNODEs learn continuous-time dynamics with states reconstructible from ob-
servations, enabling outcome prediction under alternative treatment paths.
Experiments on synthetic, semi-synthetic, and real-world clinical data demonstrate
strong performance over recent sequence models, including external validation.
1 Introduction
Sequential decision-making requires predicting how actions affect future outcomes under evolving
observations. In medicine, this arises in dynamic treatment regimes (DTRs), where treatments are
repeatedly adapted to a patient’s longitudinal history (Murphy, 2003; Robins, 2004).
A central challenge is the identification of causal treatment effects in continuous time. In observational
data, time-dependent confounding and treatment–confounder feedback arise when covariates affect
both treatment assignment and outcomes while themselves being affected by earlier treatments
(Hernán, Robins, 2023). Identification becomes even more difficult when relevant components of the
evolving disease state are latent and hidden variables affect both treatment and outcomes.
We address this problem from a state-space perspective. Under explicit structural assumptions, we
show that observability makes the latent state reconstructible from treatment-outcome histories and
thereby enables identification through a continuous-time conditional front-door adjustment, even
in the presence of hidden confounding. We derive a general adjustment formula and show that, for
temporally uncorrelated contemporaneous disturbances, it reduces to a tractable state-space formula.

effects under hidden confounding. (ii) We derive a tractable state-space adjustment under temporally

construction for causal forecasting under dynamic treatment regimes. (iii) We demonstrate strong
causal forecasting performance on synthetic, semi-synthetic, and real-world clinical data compared
with recent sequence models, including external validation.
2 Problem statement
We assume hat we observe a continuous-time multivariate outcome process Yt and a time-varying
treatment process At for times t ∈ [0, tc). Given the observed history {a[0,tc ), y[0,tc ]} with a[0,tc ) =
{At = at|0 ≤ t < tc} and y[0,tc ] = {Yt = yt|0 ≤ t ≤ tc}, the goal is to predict the probability
density of the conditional potential outcome Yt(a[tc ,tc +s)) on [tc, tc + s) under a hypothetical
treatment path a[tc ,tc +s) = {At = at|tc ≤ t < tc + s}. More precisely, we seek to identify the
conditional potential-outcome density (CPOD) PYtc +s (a[tc ,tc +s) )|Ytc ,O[0,tc ) (ytc +s|ytc , o[0,tc )), given
the last observation ytc and the history of observations and treatments o[0,tc ) = {a[0,tc ), y[0,tc )}.
In clinical applications, Yt may contain irregularly sampled laboratory values, vital signs, or imaging
embeddings, while At represents binary, categorical, or continuous treatments. Outside randomized
trials, treatment assignment depends on the evolving patient history, which induces time-dependent
confounding and treatment-confounder feedback.
We treat all measured variables as components of Yt and define outcomes, when needed, as functions
of Yt. This entails no loss of generality for the identification results below. We use outcome and
observed process interchangeably for Yt.
3 Preliminaries
3.1 Nonlinear state-space model
A continuous-time deterministic state-space model is given by
˙zt = f (zt, at) (1a), yt = h(zt) (1b), z0 = ζ (1c) (1)
where zt ∈ Rdz is a latent state, at ∈ Rda a treatment or control, and yt ∈ Rdy the observed process.
The measurement function h is not necessarily invertible, in particular when dz > dy . We assume f
is Lipschitz in z, so the system admits a unique solution for any admissible control path and initial
condition.
For a given control path, observability asks whether the latent state or, equivalently, its initial value,
is uniquely determined by the observed output over a finite horizon (Bernard, 2019). For a given
control a and initial condition z0 = ζ, let zt(a, ζ) denote the resulting state trajectory, and define
the corresponding output trajectory by yt(a, ζ) := h(zt(a, ζ)).
Definition 3.1. The system (1) is uniformly observable on an open set U ⊂ Rdz if, for any control

4.3 Prediction and training of ObsNODEs
We approximate the filtering distribution p(zt|yt, o[0,t)) with an RNN encoder, following (Chen
et al., 2018). Given the inferred state zt, forecasts under a hypothetical treatment path a[t,t+s) are
obtained by integrating (7) forward in time and projecting to the observed space.
Training is self-supervised. For each unit, we choose a decision time tc and provide observations up
to tc. The model then predicts future observations on (tc, tf ].
Observations are available only at irregular time points Tij . Let Tij (tc, tf ) = Tij ∩ (tc, tf ] denote the
observation times of component j for unit i in that forecast window (tc, tf ]. The predicted outcome
vector for unit i and outcome component j at time t is to be compared against the corresponding
observed value ydat tij in the training data. We minimize the masked squared error

5 Experiments
We evaluate ObsNODE on synthetic and semi-synthetic datasets where the ground-truth causal effect
of a prescribed treatment path on the outcome trajectories is known and, in addition, on a real world
data set with an external validation. We compare against the IGC-Net (Hess et al., 2026), SCIP-Net
(Hess, Feuerriegel, 2025) and two Treatment Effect Neural Controlled Differential Equation models
(Seedat et al., 2022): optAB for categorical treatments (Wendland et al., 2024) and doseAI for
categorical and continuous treatments (Wendland, Kschischo, 2025).
5
Forecast root mean squared error (RMSE) was scaled with the standard deviation of each dynamic
variable in the test set and is reported as a function of assimilation time (the duration of the treatment-
observation history tc) and prediction horizon (Figures 2–4). The RMSE values of these heatmaps
are given in https://github.com/JenniferJaschob/ObsNODE.git.
As an ablation study, we compare ObsNODE with an otherwise identical latent Neural ODE without
the observable normal form. For each test patient, both models assimilate the factual history up to
tc = 5 and predict outcomes under five prespecified treatment regimes. Counterfactual bias (CB) is
the signed difference between predicted and ground-truth potential outcomes, averaged over all test
patients and six prediction time points. We also evaluate the average treatment effect (ATE) of each
regime relative to a baseline treatment (Tables 2 and 4).
Results are averaged over five independent runs with disjoint training, validation, and test sets. For
synthetic and semi-synthetic data we vary the confounding strength γ. Details on data generation and
the counterfactual treatment scenarios are provided in the Appendix A.4.1 and A.4.2.

7 Conclusion and Limitations
We introduced Observable Neural ODEs (ObsNODEs), linking control-theoretic observability to
causal identifiability in continuous-time state-space models. ObsNODEs enforce observability by
construction, enable causal forecasting under alternative treatment trajectories, and perform strongly
on synthetic, semi-synthetic, and clinical data, including external validation.
The approach relies on consistency, positivity, and the stated state-space assumptions. The tractable
adjustment formula permits contemporaneous hidden confounding but assumes temporally indepen-
dent disturbances; serially correlated confounding requires the general path-space adjustment derived
in the Appendix. State reconstruction from finite, noisy observations remains approximate, and causal
accuracy cannot be assessed on clinical data without counterfactual ground truth. Future work should
address persistent confounding and uncertainty quantification.

process, sampling alternative treatment trajectories from this process, and averaging the conditional
outcome density over these trajectories. Such an implementation additionally requires estimation
of the complete treatment-path distribution and suitable path-space positivity. However, its non-
parametric estimation additionally requires path-space and mediator positivity, which may fail for
deterministic latent dynamics. Practical implementations may therefore require stochastic latent
dynamics or an augmented observable state that captures the temporally persistent confounding
process.
The ObsNODE implementation considered in this work instead uses Assumption 4.2, under which
the outcome density is independent of a′ [t,t+s) given Zt+s. The path integral then equals one, and
(13) reduces to the tractable adjustment formula (4).
A.3 Details on training the ObsNODE model
The datasets were split into training, validation, and test sets and feature-wise Z-score normalization
was applied using statistics computed on the training set only. The normalized dataset is subsequently
used for model training.
Each multivariate time series was segmented at a decision time point tc into two disjoint subsequences.
The prefix segment consists of all observations prior to tc and is used to condition the recognition
model. The model infers an initial state z0 for each individual trajectory using an an LSTM-based
recognition network (Rubanova et al., 2019).
To handle missing observations, the recognition LSTM is augmented with an explicit imputation
mechanism. The indicator function
Itij =

indicates whether the data for the j−th component of the i−th unit at time t is observed or not, The
imputation layer is defined as the transformation

where bj are learnable parameters (Jong de et al., 2019; Wendland et al., 2022). This formulation
replaces missing entries with trainable values that are optimized jointly with the LSTM parameters in
an end-to-end fashion.
The latent initial states z0 inferred by the LSTM, together with the control function at are sub-
sequently passed as inputs to the Neural Differential Equation (NODE) model. The observations
after the decision time point tc are used for loss computation and subsequent optimization. Model
parameters are optimized using the Adam optimizer.
The ObsNODE model supports both direct long-horizon forecasting and a recursive scheme, similar
in spirit to ODE-RNN (Rubanova et al., 2019). In the long-horizon setting, the state estimated by
the LSTM recognition model is used to generate a forecast over the entire prediction window. In
the recursive setting, forecasts are produced iteratively: at each step, the ODE model generates a
short-term prediction, which is appended to the observation and treatment history sequence observed
up to tc. Conditioned on this augmented sequence, the LSTM updates the latent state, which then
serves as the initial condition for the next ODE rollout. This process repeats until the full horizon is
covered, without using any ground-truth observations beyond tc. We find empirically that augmenting
the sequence with intermediate predictions can improve stability for longer forecasting horizons in
some settings.

A.5.5 Hyperparameter Tuning of NODE-Model
As a baseline model, we implemented a Neural Ordinary Differential Equation (NODE) model using
the same encoder-LSTM architecture as employed for the ObsNODE model. In contrast to the
ObsNODE formulation, the latent dynamics are modeled using a standard latent Neural ODE without

A.6 Computational efficiency
For training and hyperparameter optimization of ObsNODE we utilized two Nvidia A100 GPUs,
each with 40 GB of memory. The same setting was used for the baseline models DoseAI, OptAB and
SCIP-Net. For IGC-Net, we used a NVIDIA H100 GPU with 96 GB of memory.
Training of ObsNODE for the optimal hyperparameter values on a single Nvidia A100 GPU took 140
min for the synthetic cancer data, 49 min for the semi-synthetic data and 65 min for the real world
sepsis data.
This is similar to the training times required for the baseline models IGC-Net and SCIP-Net. Training
OptAB and DoseAI required between 40 and 74 hours, depending on the data set.
A.7 Declaration of LLM usage
The core method development in this research does not involve LLMs as any important original, or
non-standard components.
