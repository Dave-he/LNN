DS3M: Deep structured state space models for nonlinear system
identification on modern hardware* † ‡
Marco Roschkowski Hannes Gernandt
University of Wuppertal, Wuppertal, Germany
(e-mail: {roschkowski,gernandt}@uni-wuppertal.de).
Abstract
Neural state-space identification learns dynamical models from measured inputs and outputs.
SUBNET combines this task with an encoder that estimates initial states from short measurement
histories. Its continuous-time version requires many successive neural-network evaluations during
numerical integration, resulting in slow training speed which is a major limitation for practical use
cases. We propose deep structured state space models (DS3M), which combine compact nonlinear
dynamical components in parallel and in series. Custom GPU kernels implemented with Triton reduce
the launch and memory overhead of their sequential evaluation. We explain this construction, describe
a port-Hamiltonian variant, and present various benchmark comparisons of accuracy and training time.
Keywords: system identification, SUBNET, neural networks, GPU computing, structured state space
models, port-Hamiltonian systems
1 Introduction
System identification constructs a dynamical model from measured inputs and outputs. Such a model
describes how a system responds to new inputs and provides a basis for simulation, monitoring and
controller design (Schoukens and Ljung, 2019). This is useful when a model derived from physical laws is
unavailable, too expensive to evaluate, or contains unknown constitutive relations. For example, friction,
saturation and other nonlinear effects may be difficult to describe accurately from first principles but are
visible in measured trajectories.
We consider control systems of the typical form
˙x(t) = f (x(t), u(t)),
y(t) = g(x(t), u(t)). (1)
Here the input u(t) ∈ Rm and output are measured, whereas the state x(t) ∈ Rn is generally unobserved.
The identification problem is to determine a model of (1) from sampled input–output data. Besides
classical identification approaches Nelles (2020), neural networks provide flexible parameterizations of
the unknown functions f and g in (1). However, learning a state equation also requires initial states for the
simulated trajectories. SUBNET addresses this problem by learning an encoder from a short measurement
history to a model state (Beintema et al., 2021, 2023b). The same encoder and state equation are trained
on many short subsequences. This approach was extended to continuous-time models in Beintema et al.
(2023a) and to port-Hamiltonian models in Moradi et al. (2026). Recently, a variant to obtain symbolic
representations of port-Hamiltonian systems was presented in Messaoudi et al. (2026).

Accurate models can nevertheless be expensive to train. Each trajectory requires successive state
updates, and a numerical integration step can itself require several evaluations of a neural network. Small
state equations therefore generate many small, dependent computations. A graphics processing unit (GPU)
can process different trajectories in parallel, but the repeated overhead to move data between different
memory locations in the GPU can significantly slow down the computation.
Our starting point is the comparison of training workloads between training CT-SUBNET on the
Wiener-Hammerstein dataset and training ResNet50 on Imagenet, see He et al. (2016); Russakovsky et al.
(2015). This comparison is depicted in Figure 1. We can see that the latter task, a standard machine
learning task, requires significantly fewer serial model evaluations and benefits from substantially higher
batch parallelism while requiring a higher training arithmetic. In particular, training ResNet50 on Imagenet
benefits significantly more from modern hardware accelerators such as GPUs. The most important reason
for this are the sequential model evaluations during the rollout of CT-SUBNET which often requires
hundreds of serial evaluations for a single training step. The present work aims to speed up the training
time of CT-SUBNET on modern accelerator hardware. To do this, we implemented custom GPU kernels
using Triton Tillet et al. (2019). This allows to drastically reduce the number of kernel launches and thus
accelerate the training speed on GPUs by a significant margin. However, since this forces the dynamics to
be executed in a single program instance, it requires that the weights have a relatively small size to fit into
the relevant SRAM and that the neural network architecture is known in advance. Although this suffices
to match state of the art results on standard benchmark datasets Schoukens et al. (2026) and in some cases
even allows us to supersede them by additional hyperparameter tuning, this represents a limitation for
future applications on identifying larger systems. Although we do not analyze the neural scaling laws of
our method as in Roschkowski et al. (2026), our method does in principle allow to be scaled to arbitrary
model capacities while already having favorable properties on conventional benchmark datasets as shown
below.

2.1 Recap of SUBNET and CT-SUBNET
First, we recall how SUBNET learns initial states and dynamics jointly, and where the sequential
computational cost originates.
In the output-error setting considered here, the measured output is modelled as the output of a
deterministic state equation plus measurement noise. For each training subsequence, an encoder receives
a finite history of inputs and outputs and estimates the state at the start of a simulation. The estimated
state is then propagated using the recorded inputs, and the simulated outputs are compared with the
measurements. The encoder, state equation and output map are optimised jointly. This avoids measuring
the state or treating the initial state of every subsequence as an independent optimisation variable.
The typically nonlinear encoder approximates a state reconstruction map. It compresses the informa-
tion about the past that is needed to predict subsequent behaviour. The term subspace encoder connects
this construction to state reconstruction in subspace identification, see Verhaegen and Dewilde (1992);
Van Overschee and De Moor (1994).
Training uses short, possibly overlapping, subsequences sampled from the available records. Their
simulation errors are averaged over a batch. Different subsequences can be evaluated in parallel, and
truncation reduces the length of the simulations through which derivatives must be propagated. Thus,
SUBNET already provides parallelism across training windows. Within each window, however, the next
state still depends on the current state.
In CT-SUBNET, a neural network parametrises the state derivative, and a numerical integrator

produces the sampled state transition (Beintema et al., 2023a). For sampling interval ∆t > 0, we write
xk+1 = I(fϑ, xk, uk), ˙x = τ −1fϑ(x, u), (2)
where τ > 0 scales the state derivative. The map I integrates over one sampling interval and discretizes
the differential equation on the right in (2). We refer to Linares et al. (2026) for a more detailed discussion
of discretization methods. Under a zero-order hold, uk is kept constant on that interval. For example,
explicit Euler gives xk+1 = xk + (∆t/τ )fϑ(xk, uk). The ratio ∆t/τ therefore affects the size of the state
update and the numerical conditioning of training. For more general explicit methods with s stages we
require s vector-field evaluations per integration step. With one step per sample, advancing a window of
T samples takes approximately sT such evaluations.
Kernel fusion combines several operations, such as neural network evaluations and numerical inte-
gration steps, into a single GPU kernel. This reduces the overhead associated with repeatedly launching
kernels and transferring intermediate results between GPU memory locations. The state updates remain
sequential, since the computation of xk+1 depends on xk. We will now describe how we use kernel fusion
to obtain an architecture which is able to utilize the high level of parallelization of modern GPUs better.
2.2 DS3M blocks
Our starting point is to use a single kernel implemented in Triton to speed up the SUBNET rollout. Here we
use an implementation which keeps the weights inside the registers throughout the entire kernel execution,
but other designs are also possible. Since the registers can only store a fixed amount of parameters, the
size of the SUBNET parameters is limited by this approach. This issue limits future applications to larger
systems. To address it, we propose to execute the forward of H SUBNETs in parallel in a single block,
paralleling the multi-head construction in transformers, Vaswani et al. (2017). Secondly, we use a serial
interconnection of N such blocks with appropriate MLP blocks that mix the dynamics of each component.

complete the model.

2.3 Efficient evaluation with Triton
We explain how the numerical recurrence can be evaluated with fewer GPU launches and memory
transfers.
A GPU kernel is a function executed on the graphics processor. One kernel launch starts a collection of
program instances, each performing the specified operations on part of the data, typically on different batch
instances. Triton is a programming language and compiler for writing such functions using operations
on blocks of arrays (Tillet et al., 2019). In a conventional PyTorch implementation, matrix products,
activation functions and integration stages can result in many small kernel launches at every sample. Their
launch and memory overheads can become significant when the state equation is small.
Our custom kernels combine evaluations of the neural state equation and the integration steps. A time
loop can execute inside a program instance, while independent training windows and the H components
supply parallel work. Intermediate quantities can be reused in registers or other fast on-chip memory. The
benefit comes from reducing dispatch overhead and unnecessary transfers to the GPU’s main memory.
For the chosen fused implementation, efficiency depends on the size of the data that must remain
available during the computation. Large state equations increase register and shared-memory use and
may reduce the number of program instances that run concurrently. This motivates compact components.
There is a major alternative design option using the more general CUDA language Nickolls et al. (2008).
This amounts to implementing a larger kernel in which different program instances are allowed to produce
parts of the data and to communicate with each other. This design has, for example, already been used in
persistent RNNs Diamos et al. (2016).

2.4 Port-Hamiltonian dynamics
As an alternative to the standard DS3M block we also can impose a physics-based structure on each
component to incorporate dissipation into the learned state equation Cherifi et al. (2026); Moradi et al.
(2026). This is done by assuming a port-Hamiltonian structure for f (j) ϑ variant of our method. In this case,

no backward through a neural network is needed in the forward implementation. On the other hand, the

In this section, we explain the outcomes of our experiments. Each experiment is conducted on an
Nvida RTX 3090 card with PyTorch 2.11.0 + cu126 and Triton 3.6.0. We use float32 as the numerical
accuracy to implement the kernels. We compare the reported simulation errors and training times on some
standard nonlinear system identification benchmarks Schoukens et al. (2026).
Throughout the experiments, we keep the following parameters constant. Each kϑ, h(j) θ and Pψ
consists of a single linear layer. Moreover, gϑ, Yϕ are always MLPs with two hidden layers and 64
hidden units. Outside of f (j) ϑ , we always use the SiLU activation function, see Elfwing et al. (2018).

The embedding dimension is D = 16 and each layer has only one head which means H = 1. For the
subspace encoder, we use an MLP with two hidden layers and hidden dimension 64. We follow the

Kingma and Ba (2014), with initial learning rate 0.001, weight decay 0, gradient clipping 0.001 which is
